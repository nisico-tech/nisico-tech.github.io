How much calcium, vitamin D and protein do you need with osteopenia?

Calcium, vitamin D and protein targets for women over 50 from bone-health bodies, with food examples and a sample day that reaches 1,200 mg of calcium.

By the Sturdy team · Updated 2026-11-02

For women over 50, the Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation recommends 1,200 mg of calcium a day, counting food and supplements together, and 800 to 1,000 IU of vitamin D a day for adults aged 50 and over. For protein, the standard recommended allowance is 0.8 g per kilogram of body weight, and an expert group on older adults recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kilogram for people over 65. Food can cover most of your calcium and protein; ask your doctor whether you also need a supplement, especially for vitamin D.

General information, not medical or dietary advice. If you have kidney disease, a history of kidney stones, or take other medicines, ask your doctor, pharmacist or a dietitian before changing your diet or adding supplements.

Calcium: how much, and where it comes from

The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation (BHOF) gives 1,200 mg a day for women aged 51 and older. That figure is your total from food and supplements, so it helps to know roughly how much your meals already give you before you add a tablet.

BHOF lists these estimated amounts per serving:

FoodServingCalcium (estimated)
Collard greens, frozen8 oz360 mg
Sardines, canned with bones3 oz325 mg
Plain low-fat yogurt6 oz310 mg
Milk (skim, low-fat or whole)8 oz300 mg
Orange juice, calcium-fortified8 oz300 mg

BHOF notes that these amounts are estimates and vary by product, so check the label of what you actually buy.

What 1,200 mg can look like in a day

Using the figures above: a 6 oz pot of plain yogurt at breakfast (310 mg), a glass of milk during the day (300 mg), sardines on toast at lunch (325 mg) and a side of collard greens at dinner (360 mg) add up to about 1,295 mg. You do not need these exact foods; the point is that three or four calcium-rich servings spread across the day can get you close to the target. If one day falls short, there is no need to make it up in a single meal; simply aim for a better day tomorrow and look at the pattern over a week.

Tip: write down one ordinary day of eating, then add up the calcium from labels. You will see whether you are short by one serving or by more, and which meal is the easiest to change.

Vitamin D

BHOF recommends 800 to 1,000 IU of vitamin D a day for adults aged 50 and older. Unlike calcium, it is hard to count from a food diary, so whether you need a supplement, and how much, is a question for your doctor, who may also suggest checking your blood level.

Protein: why it matters and how much

Osteoporosis Canada explains that protein forms the structure in bone that holds calcium and helps keep muscles strong, and that weaker muscles can affect balance and lead to falls. It also notes that protein intake is associated with greater bone mass and fewer fractures when calcium intake is adequate.

How much? Osteoporosis Canada gives the Recommended Dietary Allowance of 0.8 g of protein per kilogram of body weight (0.364 g per pound). The PROT-AGE Study Group, writing in the Journal of the American Medical Directors Association in 2013, recommends that adults over 65 eat on average at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kilogram a day.

These are simple multiplications of the published figures, not a personal target. If you have kidney disease, do not increase your protein without talking to your doctor.

Good protein sources

Osteoporosis Canada lists meat, poultry and fish; eggs and milk products (which also bring calcium); beans, lentils and tofu; and nut and seed butters, nuts and seeds. A practical habit is to include one of these at every meal rather than trying to catch up at dinner. Yogurt, milk and canned fish with bones count twice: they add protein and calcium.

If you do not eat dairy

Three of the five foods in the BHOF list above are not dairy: collard greens, canned sardines with their bones, and calcium-fortified orange juice. If you use a plant-based drink or tofu, look for products that say they are calcium-fortified and read the amount per serving on the label, because it differs a lot between brands. For protein, Osteoporosis Canada's list already includes beans, lentils, tofu, nuts and seeds, so a dairy-free diet can still cover both, it just takes a little more planning.

Food first, then supplements

Because the calcium figure includes supplements, a common approach is to see what your food already provides and then ask your doctor whether a supplement should cover the gap. Bring a simple food record to the appointment: it makes the conversation faster and more specific.

Questions for your doctor

  • Roughly how much calcium do I get from food, and do I need a supplement on top?
  • Should my vitamin D level be checked?
  • Is there any reason I should not increase my protein?
  • Does any of my medication change these targets?
  • Would it help to see a registered dietitian, for example if I avoid dairy or eat very little?
