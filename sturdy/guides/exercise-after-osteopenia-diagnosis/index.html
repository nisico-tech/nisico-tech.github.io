What exercise is safe after an osteopenia or osteoporosis diagnosis?

What the UK consensus on exercise for osteoporosis recommends: strength, impact, balance and posture, plus what changes after a spine fracture.

By the Sturdy team · Updated 2026-11-02

Most people with osteopenia or osteoporosis can exercise, and the UK expert consensus on the subject says they should be encouraged to do more, not less. In short: strength training on two or three days a week, some impact (or brisk walking if you have had a spine fracture), balance work at least twice a week, and exercises for the muscles of your back, while avoiding deep forward bending of the spine, especially with a weight in your hands. What is right for you depends on your fracture history, so check with your doctor or a physical therapist before you start.

This is general information, not medical advice. It does not replace your doctor, your physical therapist or your medication, and no exercise plan can promise a change in your next DEXA result.

Where this advice comes from

In 2022 a UK expert group published “Strong, steady and straight”, a consensus statement on physical activity and exercise for osteoporosis, in the British Journal of Sports Medicine. It is organised around three goals: strong bones, steady balance to avoid falls, and a straight posture to protect the spine. It also says that exercise is not associated with significant harm in people with osteoporosis and that the benefits generally outweigh the risks.

The Royal Osteoporosis Society gives the same message for patients: most people with osteoporosis can exercise safely, the best approach combines impact and strength exercise, and you should build up gradually.

If you have had a spine fracture

A vertebral (spine) fracture changes the advice. The consensus keeps impact at the level of brisk walking, recommends a referral to a physiotherapist for back-strengthening and endurance work tailored to you, and encourages you to stay active for pain and wellbeing. It also says that early advice on how to move and lift after a painful fracture helps reduce fear. If you are not sure, ask your doctor directly whether any of your scans or X-rays have shown a spine fracture.

The Royal Osteoporosis Society adds that you should speak to your doctor or physiotherapist first if you have had many broken bones or spinal fractures, are recovering from a break, or have any other concern about your situation.

How to move safely every day

The consensus avoids long lists of banned activities and prefers teaching safe ways to move. Its main points:

  • Avoid deep forward bending of the spine, especially under load. Sustained, repeated or end-of-range flexion should be changed or avoided, and loaded flexion should be avoided.
  • Bend from the hips. Use a hip hinge to pick things up, brace your stomach muscles, and move smoothly within a comfortable range.
  • “Think straight.” Keep your upper back straight and your neck in line with your spine.
  • Swap risky class moves. In Pilates or yoga, consider alternatives to “roll down” and “curl up” exercises.

What about heavy lifting?

You may have read about the LIFTMOR trial (Journal of Bone and Mineral Research, 2018). It randomised 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass to eight months of either twice-weekly 30-minute sessions of high-intensity resistance and impact training (5 sets of 5 repetitions at over 85% of the most they could lift once) or a home-based, low-intensity programme. The high-intensity group did better on bone density and physical function measures, and only one minor adverse event (a lower back spasm) was reported.

Two things matter here. First, the training was delivered under close supervision, so the study does not show that the same programme is safe to do alone at home. Second, it is one study of one group of women; it cannot tell you what will happen to your own scan. If heavy lifting appeals to you, build up to it gradually and ask about a session with a physiotherapist or qualified trainer first.

An example first week

If your doctor or physiotherapist is happy for you to start, a beginner week might look like this. It is an example, not a prescription:

  1. Day 1, strength (20 to 30 minutes): sit-to-stand from a chair, wall push-ups, heel raises holding a counter, a hip hinge with a broomstick to practise the movement, and a back-extensor exercise lying on your front.
  2. Day 2: a brisk walk and a few minutes of balance practice, such as standing on one leg next to a kitchen counter.
  3. Day 3, strength: repeat day 1. If every set felt easy, add a few repetitions next time before you add weight.
  4. Day 5, strength: repeat again, then rest.

Stop and contact your doctor if you feel sudden or sharp back pain, or pain that does not settle.
