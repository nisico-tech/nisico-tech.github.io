What happens at the US citizenship interview?

A step-by-step look at the naturalization interview: the oath, the N-400 review, the English reading, writing and speaking tests, civics, and retests.

By the Oathready team · Updated 2026-11-02

At your naturalization interview, a USCIS officer places you under oath, goes through the answers on your Form N-400 with you, and gives you the naturalization test: an English test (speaking, reading and writing) and a civics test asked out loud (USCIS; USCIS Policy Manual). Your speaking is judged from how you understand and answer during the interview itself. If you fail one part, you are retested only on that part, between 60 and 90 days later. Here is what each step looks like, using USCIS’s own pages.

This guide explains the format only. It is not legal advice and does not tell you how to answer questions about your own case. If you are unsure about anything in your application, talk to an immigration attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative before your interview. A directory of nonprofit legal services is at immigrationadvocates.org.

1. The oath

USCIS guidance says the officer “places the alien under oath at the start of the interview” (Policy Manual, Vol. 12, Part B, Ch. 3). Expect to be asked to promise to tell the truth before any other question.

2. Questions about your N-400

The officer “interviews the alien on the questions and responses in the alien’s naturalization application”, and the record can include amendments to your answers that the officer makes during the interview (USCIS Policy Manual). Expect questions about the facts you wrote: your address, work, travel, family, and the long list of “Have you ever…” questions.

This part is also your speaking test. USCIS says your ability to speak and understand English is determined by the officer during the eligibility interview on your N-400 (USCIS). The Policy Manual says it is based on your ability to respond to questions normally asked in the interview (Policy Manual, Vol. 12, Part E, Ch. 2).

You can ask the officer to repeat

The Policy Manual states that “an officer should repeat and rephrase questions until the officer is satisfied” (source). Saying “Can you repeat that, please?” or “Could you say it another way?” is normal and expected.

3. The reading test

You are shown up to three sentences and must read one of three out loud correctly (USCIS; Policy Manual). USCIS publishes the reading vocabulary list on its study page.

4. The writing test

You must write one of three sentences in a way the officer understands (Policy Manual). USCIS also publishes a writing vocabulary list.

5. The civics test

The civics test is given orally: the officer asks, you answer (Policy Manual). Which version you take depends on when you filed:

N-400 filedTestQuestions askedTo pass
Before Oct. 20, 20252008 civics test (list of 100)Up to 106 correct (5 incorrect means failing)
On or after Oct. 20, 20252025 civics test (list of 128)Up to 2012 correct (9 incorrect means failing)

Source: USCIS, Study for the Test. Some answers depend on where you live (your senators, your representative, your governor), so check the current names before your interview.

6. If you do not pass a part

USCIS says you will be retested on the portion you failed, between 60 and 90 days from your first interview (USCIS). The officer must only retest the areas you failed; if you fail a part a second time, the Policy Manual says the officer must deny the application for not meeting the educational requirements (Policy Manual). So a retest is a real second chance, and the time before it is best spent on that one part.

Exceptions you may qualify for

  • 50/20 and 55/15: if you were 50 or older with 20 years as a permanent resident, or 55 or older with 15 years, when you filed, you are exempt from the English requirement but still take civics, and you may take it in your language with an interpreter you bring (USCIS).
  • 65/20: if you are 65 or older with 20 years as a permanent resident, you get a 10-question civics test from a special list of 20, in the language of your choice (USCIS).
  • Medical exception: Form N-648, completed by a licensed medical or osteopathic doctor or licensed clinical psychologist (USCIS).

Whether an exception applies to you is a question for an attorney or accredited representative.

How to prepare

  1. Use the official materials. USCIS’s study page has the civics questions, reading and writing vocabulary and practice tests, free (USCIS).
  2. Practise out loud, not just reading. Civics is oral, and speaking is judged through the conversation.
  3. Learn the words on your own application. Know what each question on your N-400 means. Your answers should be the true answers you gave; if something in your application worries you, ask a lawyer before the interview, not the app or a friend.
  4. Practise full sentences and asking for a repeat. Both are normal at the interview.
  5. Do a full rehearsal. Run through all the steps above in order at least once, so the format is familiar.
