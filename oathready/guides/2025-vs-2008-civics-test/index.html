2025 or 2008 civics test: which one will you take?

Your N-400 filing date decides your civics test. How the 2025 test (128 questions) differs from the 2008 test (100 questions), and the 65/20 rule.

By the Oathready team · Updated 2026-11-02

It depends on the date USCIS received your Form N-400. If you filed before October 20, 2025, you take the 2008 civics test. If you filed on or after October 20, 2025, you take the 2025 civics test (USCIS). The two tests use different question lists and different pass rules, so studying the wrong one wastes time. Here are the differences, with USCIS as the source for each.

The two tests side by side

Source: USCIS, Study for the Test and The Naturalization Interview and Test.

In both versions the test is oral: the officer asks and you answer out loud (USCIS Policy Manual). The test can end early once you have enough correct answers, or enough incorrect ones, so you may not hear every question.

How to find your filing date

Look for the date USCIS received your N-400 on the receipt notice USCIS sent you. If you are not sure which date counts in your case, ask an attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative; a nonprofit directory is at immigrationadvocates.org.

What stays the same

The change is in civics only. Both groups still take the English test:

  • Speaking: judged by the officer during your N-400 interview (USCIS).
  • Reading: read one of three sentences correctly (USCIS).
  • Writing: write one of three sentences correctly (USCIS).

And the retest rule is the same: if you fail a part, you are retested on that part between 60 and 90 days after your first interview (USCIS).

The 65/20 special consideration

If you are 65 or older and have been a permanent resident for 20 years or more, USCIS gives you a 10-question civics test drawn from a special list of 20 questions, based on either the 2008 or 2025 test. You may study only the 20 questions marked with an asterisk, and you may take the test in the language of your choice (USCIS). USCIS’s exceptions page says that applicants who test in their own language must bring an interpreter fluent in both English and that language (USCIS).

The 50/20 and 55/15 exceptions remove the English requirement but not civics (USCIS). Whether you qualify depends on your age and years as a permanent resident at the time you filed. Check with a legal professional if you are unsure.

Study tips for each version

If you take the 2008 test

  • Study the 100-question list on USCIS’s site, and make sure the study app or book you use is the 2008 version.
  • Six correct out of up to ten means each question counts a lot: practise until you can answer every question out loud, not just recognise the answer.

If you take the 2025 test

  • Use the 128-question list. Some older study tools may still show only the 100-question list, so check the number before you start.
  • Twenty questions means more ground to cover. Short daily sessions over several weeks are easier than cramming.
  • Practise a full mock with the real stop rule: 12 correct to pass, 9 incorrect to fail.

For both

  • Some answers change with elections or depend on where you live (senators, representative, governor). Check the current names close to your interview.
  • Listen to the questions as well as reading them. On the day, you will hear them, not see them.
  • Official flashcards, audio and practice tests are free on the USCIS study page (USCIS).

How to practise so it sticks

  1. Say the answer before you look. Recognising an answer on a card is easier than saying it from memory. The real test asks you to say it.
  2. Short daily sessions. Ten or fifteen minutes a day, coming back to the questions you missed, works better than one long night.
  3. Mix the order. If you always study in the same order, you start remembering the sequence instead of the answers.
  4. Practise with another person. Ask a family member or a class partner to read questions in English at a normal speed. Hearing a different voice is good preparation.
  5. Do a full mock with the stop rule. Count correct and incorrect answers as the officer would, so the length of the test feels familiar.

Some public libraries and adult schools run citizenship classes. Ask your local library or adult school what it offers.

Before you study anything, check which test you are taking. The number on the question list (100 or 128) tells you at a glance whether a study tool matches your test.
