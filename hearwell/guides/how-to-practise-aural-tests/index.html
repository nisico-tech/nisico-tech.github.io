How to practise for music exam aural tests at home

A five-minute daily aural routine for Grades 1 to 5, how to practise each test type, help for children who hate singing, and how to use the last four weeks.

By the Hearwell team · Updated 2026-11-02

Practise aural little and often: five to ten minutes on most days, one short exercise of each test type, rather than a long session the week before the exam. Start with the pulse, then spotting changes, then describing the music, and add singing or pitch work in small, comfortable steps. In the last few weeks, run full mock tests in the board's order, without stopping between questions. If your child dreads singing, start with humming or playing the answer on their instrument and move towards their voice gradually.

Why little and often

Aural skills are listening habits, and in the exam you answer straight after hearing each example. Short daily practice keeps the habits fresh and keeps aural from becoming the scary part left until last. It also fits around instrument practice: a few minutes before or after scales is enough.

A five-minute daily routine

MinutePractiseHow
1PulsePut on any piece of music and clap or tap the beat, a little stronger on the first beat of each bar. Say whether it feels like 2, 3 or 4.
2Spot the changeA parent or sibling plays or sings a short phrase twice, changing one note or one rhythm the second time. Your child says where it changed and whether it was the rhythm or a note.
3Higher or lower, intervalsPlay two notes; higher or lower? At Trinity Grade 3 and above, practise naming the interval.
4Describe itPlay a short extract and ask one question: loud or quiet? Smooth or detached? Does it get louder? Major or minor?
5Sing or play backPlay a short phrase; your child hums it, sings it or plays it back on their instrument.

A parent does not need to read music for most of this: any piano app, keyboard or recording can provide the examples, and the questions are about what you hear.

Practising each type of test

Pulse and metre

Trinity asks candidates at every grade from 1 to 5 to clap the pulse while stressing the strong beat, and at Grade 5 to name the time signature, according to its published parameters. Practise with real music in different tempos. Count “ONE two three” out loud first, then clap without counting.

Spotting a change

Start with obvious changes (a long note made short) and make them smaller over time. At the higher Trinity grades the candidate has a copy of the melody and must find the bar that changed, so older children should practise following a printed line while listening.

Describing the music

Build a small word bank and use it every day: forte, piano, crescendo, diminuendo, legato, staccato, and a few words for character and tempo. Trinity's Grade 1 questions ask for forte or piano and legato or staccato; by Grade 2 the dynamics vary within the melody. Encourage full answers: “It started quietly and got louder towards the end.”

Major, minor and cadences

Play the same short tune in major and minor and ask which is which. At Trinity Grade 4 the final cadence is perfect or imperfect; at Grade 5 it can also be plagal or interrupted. Let your child hear each cadence type many times before asking them to name it.

Singing back and sight-singing

ABRSM's test list for Grades 1 to 5 includes echoes, melodic repetition and sight-singing, according to its Aural Trainer app listing. Trinity says its candidates are not required to sing. For singing tasks, practise with short phrases of three to five notes and lengthen them slowly.

If your child hates singing

Singing is the part some children find hardest, and a UK piano teacher writing on Pianodao has described candidates who are overwhelmed by it. A gentle ladder helps:

  1. Play the phrase back on their own instrument.
  2. Hum it with the mouth closed.
  3. Whistle it or sing it quietly to “la”.
  4. Sing it quietly with no one watching.
  5. Sing it out, as in the exam.

Let your child choose when to move up a step, and practise somewhere private. Praise matching the shape of the melody, not a perfect voice. The same article reports that ABRSM has non-singing tests created for candidates with selective mutism; if singing is a genuine barrier, ask your teacher or the board what applies to your child.

The last four weeks

  • Mock the whole test once or twice a week, in the board's order, with no pauses, ideally with someone your child does not practise with every day.
  • Use the board's own practice material, such as Trinity's Aural Tests from 2017 books, so the format is familiar.
  • Rehearse recovering from a wrong answer: say “That's fine, next one” and carry on. Keeping going matters more than any single question.
  • Tell the teacher which test type still feels shaky so they can spend a few minutes on it in lessons.

Adults taking a grade

Everything above works for adult candidates too. If you practise in a shared office or at home in the evening, use headphones, hum instead of singing, and tap instead of clapping; switch to full voice and clapping for the last few weeks of mocks.
