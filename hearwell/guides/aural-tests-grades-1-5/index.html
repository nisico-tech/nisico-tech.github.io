What happens in the aural test of a music exam, Grades 1 to 5?

What the ABRSM and Trinity aural tests ask at Grades 1 to 5, from the boards' own published material, plus what to check before you start preparing.

By the Hearwell team · Updated 2026-11-02

The aural test checks how well you hear and understand music without the score: keeping the pulse, noticing what changed, telling higher from lower or major from minor, and describing things like dynamics and articulation. The two big UK boards run it differently: at ABRSM it is part of the practical grade exams and includes singing tasks, while at Trinity it is one of four supporting tests you can choose, the examiner plays a single example at the piano, and candidates are not required to sing. Always check the current syllabus for your board, instrument and grade before the exam.

Trinity College London: Grades 1 to 5

Trinity's aural test parameters page says the test asks candidates to recognise musical features by ear, such as metre and pulse, pitch and performance characteristics, that all questions are based on a single musical example played at the piano by the examiner, and that candidates are not required to sing. Trinity's page on supporting tests says candidates in face-to-face exams take two supporting tests, chosen from aural, sight reading, improvisation and musical knowledge, with sight reading becoming compulsory from Grade 6.

Trinity's published requirements and parameters (from 2017) set out each grade:

GradeWhat is playedWhat you do
1A 4-bar melody in a major key, in 2 or 3 timeClap the pulse on the third playing, stressing the strong beat; say whether it was forte or piano and legato or staccato; say whether the last note of the first two bars is higher or lower than the first; spot where a rhythm or pitch change happens
2A 4-bar melody, major or minor, in 2 or 3 timeClap the pulse; describe the dynamics, which vary; legato or staccato; higher or lower; say where the change is and whether it was rhythm or pitch
3A 4-bar melody, major or minor, in 3 or 4 timeClap the pulse on the second playing; major or minor; name the interval between the first two notes by number (second to sixth); with a copy of the melody, find the bar that changed and say whether it was rhythm or pitch
4A 4-bar harmonised piece, major or minor, in 4 or 6 timeClap the pulse; major or minor; final cadence perfect or imperfect; name the interval including its quality (minor or major second up to sixths); with a copy, find the bar with the rhythm change and the bar with the pitch change
5An 8-bar harmonised piece, major or minor, in 2, 3, 4 or 6 timeClap the pulse and name the time signature; describe the changing tonality; final cadence perfect, plagal, imperfect or interrupted; name an interval from the melody, up to an octave; with a copy, find the bars with the rhythm change and the pitch change

Trinity also points candidates to its practice books, Aural Tests from 2017, available from its shop or music shops.

ABRSM: Grades 1 to 5

ABRSM's aural tests are part of its practical grade exams. ABRSM's own Aural Trainer app for Grades 1 to 5 lists the test types it covers at these grades: pulse and metre, echoes, differences, melodic repetition, sight-singing, musical features, rhythmic repetition and metre. The listing does not say which test belongs to which grade. That, the exact wording of each test and how it is marked are set out in the ABRSM syllabus for your instrument, so read the aural section for your grade there.

Singing is part of the ABRSM tests. A UK piano teacher writing on Pianodao in 2022 quoted the ABRSM Grade 4 instruction “Please sing the notes” and described how the singing element can overwhelm anxious candidates. The same article reports that ABRSM has a set of non-singing aural tests created for candidates with selective mutism. If singing is a real barrier for your child, ask your teacher, or ABRSM directly, what options apply in your case.

How the two boards compare

  • Pulse. Trinity asks you to clap the pulse at every grade; ABRSM's list includes pulse and metre.
  • Spotting a change. Trinity plays the melody twice with a change of rhythm or pitch; ABRSM's list includes a “differences” test.
  • Talking about the music. Trinity asks about dynamics and articulation from Grade 1; ABRSM's list includes a “musical features” test.
  • Pitch. Trinity uses higher/lower and interval questions; ABRSM's list includes echoes, melodic repetition and sight-singing.

How the test feels on the day

In the exam you answer straight after listening, to an examiner rather than a parent. A child who can do every task at home may still freeze in that setting. Practising under exam-like conditions, with someone playing the examples and asking the questions in order, helps make that setting familiar.

What to check before you start preparing

  1. Which board, which instrument and which grade.
  2. For Trinity: whether you are taking aural as one of your two supporting tests.
  3. The current syllabus page for that grade, and the date it applies from.
  4. The vocabulary your teacher wants you to use for dynamics, articulation and character.
  5. For ABRSM: whether singing is going to be a problem, early enough to ask about options.
