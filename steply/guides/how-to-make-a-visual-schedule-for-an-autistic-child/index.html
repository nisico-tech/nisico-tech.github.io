How to make a visual schedule for an autistic child at home

A step-by-step guide for parents: choose a routine, break it into steps, pick pictures your child understands, and teach the schedule so it sticks.

By the Steply team · Updated 2026-11-02

To make a visual schedule for an autistic child, pick one routine that is hard every day, break it into 3 to 6 small steps, show each step as a clear picture (a photo of your child's own things works best for many young children), put the pictures in order where your child can see them, and go through it together the same way each day, letting your child mark each step as done. Start small, keep it predictable, and add more only once the first routine works.

Visual supports are one of the most studied tools in autism. The 2020 review by the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice lists visual supports as an evidence-based practice, based on 104 single-case and 2 group-design studies, with results for children from early intervention age through young adulthood. The UK's NICE guideline on autism in under-19s advises "visual supports, for example, words, pictures or symbols that are meaningful for the child". This guide turns that into steps you can try at home this week.

What a visual schedule is (and what it is not)

A visual schedule shows a sequence: what happens first, what comes next, and when the routine is finished. The AFIRM visual supports module from the University of North Carolina describes three common kinds:

  • First/Then board: two pictures, "first this, then that". The simplest place to start.
  • Routine schedule: the steps of one routine, such as getting dressed or bedtime.
  • Day schedule: the main parts of the whole day, such as breakfast, school, park, dinner.

A schedule is a way to show information. It is not a behavior plan on its own, and it is not a test your child passes or fails. Research reviews are honest about this: a 2015 review of visual activity schedules found most good-quality studies showed them to be effective, but nearly all of those studies were small single-case designs. Think of a schedule as one helpful tool, used alongside the advice of your child's therapist or teacher.

Step 1: Choose one routine to start with

Pick the routine that causes the most friction and happens every day. For many families that is the morning, leaving the house, bath time or bedtime. Starting with one routine lets your child learn what the schedule means before it is everywhere.

Good first routines are short (under 15 minutes), happen at the same time each day, and end in something your child enjoys.

Step 2: Break it into small, concrete steps

Write the routine down the way it really happens in your home. Then cut it into 3 to 6 steps. Each step should be one action your child can see: "toilet", "brush teeth", "pajamas", "story", "lights out". If a step keeps going wrong, it may be too big. "Get dressed" can become "pants", "shirt", "socks".

Step 3: Choose pictures your child understands

Pictures can be photos, drawings, symbols or written words. What matters is that your child recognizes them. For young or minimally verbal children, color photos are often the clearest: in a 2015 study, minimally verbal autistic children could use all the picture types tested, but did best with color photographs, the most realistic kind. A photo of your child's own toothbrush in your own bathroom is easier to connect with real life than a generic icon.

  • Use one picture per step, large and uncluttered.
  • Add a short word under each picture, even if your child does not read yet.
  • Keep the same picture for the same step every day.
  • Older or reading children may prefer words, or words with small icons.

Step 4: Decide on paper, screen or both

There is no single right format. In a study comparing book and tablet schedules, children learned at similar speeds with both, and which one each child preferred varied from child to child. A 2025 systematic review of digital schedules found only 17 studies with 58 participants in total, so the research on apps is still small. In practice:

  • Paper is great on the fridge, in the bathroom, or for days without screens.
  • A phone or tablet is easy to change on the spot, travels with you, and can read steps aloud.
  • Many families use both: a printed bedtime strip on the wall, and a phone for outings and surprises.

Step 5: Teach the schedule, then step back

Children usually need to be shown how to use a schedule. In a classic study of photographic activity schedules, autistic children were taught with hands-on guidance that was gradually faded, and they went on to follow new photo sequences on their own. A simple version at home:

  1. Go to the schedule together and point to the first picture. Say one short phrase: "First, toilet."
  2. Do the step together. Help as much as needed at first.
  3. Go back to the schedule and let your child mark the step done: move the card to a "done" pocket, flip it over, or tap Done.
  4. Point to the next picture. Repeat.
  5. Over days and weeks, give less help: point instead of guiding, then just wait.
Talk less, show more. The picture carries the message, so you do not have to repeat the instruction.

Step 6: Keep it predictable, and plan for changes

NICE lists "changes to routines" and "the absence of predictability and structure" among the things that can lead to behavior that challenges, and recommends "increasing structure and minimising unpredictability" (NICE CG170). Use the schedule the same way each time, and when something has to change, show the change on the schedule before it happens instead of only saying it. Common problems and simple fixes

ProblemTry this
My child ignores the scheduleMake it shorter, put it at their eye level, and use it at the start of every step, not only when things go wrong.
They skip to the fun stepShow only the current and next step for a while, like a First/Then board.
They get upset when a step is removedShow the change before it happens and put something they like after it.
It works at home but not elsewhereTake the same pictures with you, on paper or on a phone.

When to ask for professional help

If routines regularly end in long meltdowns, self-injury, or your child seems in pain or very anxious, talk to your child's doctor, occupational therapist, speech-language therapist or behavior specialist. A visual schedule can support their plan, but it does not replace it.
