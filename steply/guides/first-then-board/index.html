First/Then board: what it is and how to use it with your child

What a First/Then board is, how to make one on paper or a phone, how to use it step by step, and the common mistakes that make it stop working.

By the Steply team · Updated 2026-11-02

A First/Then board is a simple visual support with two pictures side by side. The First picture shows what is happening now; the Then picture shows what is planned next, for example "First shoes, then park." It makes the order of everyday activities visible, so your child can see it instead of only hearing it. It tends to work best when the first card is short, the second is something your child looks forward to, the pictures and words are ones your child understands, and the "then" really happens.

First/Then is one of the visual schedule types described in the AFIRM visual supports module from the University of North Carolina: "the sequencing of two events to help a student understand what comes next." Visual supports as a whole are recognized as an evidence-based practice for autistic children in the 2020 NCAEP review. Below is how to make one, how to use it day to day, and the mistakes that make it stop working.

Why a First/Then board helps

  • It answers "what next?" Many autistic children find the unknown hard. A 2020 review found a consistent link between anxiety and finding uncertainty hard to tolerate in autistic people. A board shows what comes next.
  • It lasts longer than words. A spoken instruction is gone in a second; a picture stays there while your child is thinking.
  • It shows the end of the hard part. The "then" card shows that the hard part has an end.
  • It is fast. Two cards take seconds to set up, which makes it easy to use in the middle of the day.

How to make a First/Then board

On paper

  1. Take a strip of card about the size of your hand. Draw a line down the middle and write "First" on the left and "Then" on the right.
  2. Print or draw pictures of common tasks (shoes, bath, car, toilet, teeth) and favorite things (park, tablet, snack, trampoline, a favorite toy).
  3. Laminate them or cover them with tape, and add hook-and-loop dots so cards can be swapped.
  4. Keep the board where transitions happen, such as by the front door or the bathroom.

On a phone or tablet

A phone is handy when you are out, or when you need a picture you do not have on paper. You can take a photo of the real thing, such as the shop entrance or your car, in a few seconds. In a study comparing book and tablet schedules, children learned at similar speeds with both, and preferences varied from child to child, so use whichever works for yours.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Bring the board to your child. Hold it where they can see it, in the way that works for them.
  2. Point and say it short. Point to each card: "First shoes. Then park." Avoid long explanations.
  3. Do the first task together if needed. Show, help or do it side by side. The board is support, not a test, and it is fine to adapt it to your child.
  4. Mark it done. Remove or flip the "first" card, or let your child tap Done. Seeing it disappear matters.
  5. Keep the board true. If the park is on the board, go to the park. If plans change, update the Then picture first and show the change in a way your child understands.
Only put on the "then" card something you can actually give right after. A "then" that does not happen teaches your child that the board cannot be trusted.

Choosing the right pair

Doctor visitPlayground on the way home

The "then" does not have to be a treat. For many children a calm, predictable next step, such as "then home" or "then quiet time", is enough.

Common mistakes

  • The first task is too long. "First clean your room" can feel endless. Break it down: "First blocks in the box".
  • Using it only when things go wrong. If the board appears only during a meltdown, it can start to feel like a threat. Use it on good days too.
  • Changing the "then" mid-way. If you must change it, show the new card before the first task starts.
  • Too many words. Let the pictures do the talking.

What the research says it can and cannot do

A First/Then board is a clear way to show information, but research suggests pictures alone do not always prevent upset. In a small 2009 study with two autistic children, visual schedules alone did not reduce problem behavior when moving from a favorite activity to a less-wanted one; what helped was a structured behavior plan. If transitions regularly end in meltdowns, a behavior specialist, occupational therapist or your child's doctor can help you work out why, and the board can then become part of that plan.

Moving beyond two cards

Once your child knows the board, you can add a third card ("first, then, then"), and later move to a full routine schedule. Our guide on how to make a visual schedule for an autistic child explains that next step.
