How to start a task you keep avoiding when you have ADHD

Shrink the first step, pick the moment, set a short timer you are allowed to stop. A practical, shame-free way to start avoided tasks with ADHD.

By the Tinystart team · Updated 2026-11-02

To start a task you keep avoiding, make the first step so small and so concrete that it takes two to five minutes and needs no decision: "open the folder", not "do taxes". Decide exactly when you will do it, set a short timer, and give yourself explicit permission to stop when it rings. Starting is the hard part; once you are moving, continuing is often easier. If you have ADHD, this is not a character flaw. It is a known pattern, and you can build scaffolding around it.

Why avoided tasks are so hard to start

The NHS lists "finding it hard to organise your time" and "finding it hard to follow instructions or finish tasks" among the inattentive symptoms of ADHD in adults. Many people describe something more specific: they know exactly what to do, they even want it done, and they still cannot begin.

A few things are usually going on at once:

  • The task is fuzzy. "Sort out insurance" is not an action. Your brain has to plan, decide and start all at once, and that is where it stalls.
  • The reward is far away. Research on delay discounting looks at how much people prefer a smaller reward now over a bigger one later. A 2016 meta-analysis by Jackson and MacKillop found this preference was significantly stronger in people with ADHD than in controls. The relief of a paid bill next month competes badly with anything that feels good right now.
  • The task has become emotional. After a few days of avoiding it, the task carries guilt. Opening it means facing the guilt too, so avoiding it gets easier each day.

None of this means you are lazy. It means the usual advice, "just start", skips the part that is actually hard.

Step 1: Shrink the first step until it feels almost silly

Write the task down, then ask: what is the very first physical thing I would do? Keep asking until the answer takes under five minutes and needs no thinking.

The first step is allowed to be "open it and just look at it". That counts. Looking is often the step you have been avoiding.

Step 2: Decide when, in an if-then sentence

Vague intentions ("I'll do it later") are easy to slide past. A plan that names the moment is harder to miss. Psychologists call these implementation intentions: "When X happens, I will do Y." A meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) found that forming if-then plans had a positive effect on reaching goals across many studies and kinds of goals.

Some examples:

  • "When I sit down with my coffee, I will open the tax folder."
  • "When I finish lunch, I will search for the insurer's email."
  • "When I close my laptop at 5, I will write one sentence of the reply."

Pick a moment that already happens every day. You are attaching the tiny step to something you do anyway.

Step 3: Use a short timer, and mean it when you say you can stop

Set a timer for two, five or ten minutes. Tell yourself, honestly: when it rings, I can stop and that is a success. This matters. If the timer is secretly a trick to make you work for an hour, part of you knows, and starting gets harder next time.

Often, when the timer ends, you will want to keep going. Great. Add another five minutes. But if you stop, the task is no longer untouched. You have opened it, and the next start will be smaller.

Tip: Make the end of the session easy to pick up. Before you stop, write the next tiny step on a sticky note or in the task itself, for example "Next: click the payment link". Future you does not have to decide anything.

Step 4: Lower the friction around the task

  • Put the task where you will trip over it. The form on the keyboard. The tab already open.
  • Remove one decision. Choose the pen, the template, the first line of the email in advance.
  • Pair it with something you like. A favourite drink or playlist that you only have while doing admin.
  • Try working near someone. Some people with ADHD find it easier to start with another person present, in the room or on a video call. This is often called body doubling. CHADD notes that there has not been significant research to validate the benefits and that it does not work for everyone, so treat it as something to try, not a fix.

Step 5: Expect some days not to work

Some days the timer rings and nothing happened. That is information, not failure. Ask: was the step still too big? Was the moment wrong? Make it smaller and try again tomorrow. You do not need to catch up on the days you missed. Just start the next tiny step.

What does not usually help

  • Another big to-do list. A list of 40 things makes every item feel urgent and none of them start-able.
  • Waiting for motivation. For avoided tasks, motivation tends to arrive after you start, not before.
  • Punishing yourself. Harsh self-talk adds guilt to the task, which makes it heavier to open.

When to talk to a professional

If trouble starting and finishing things is affecting your work, money, studies or relationships, it is worth talking to a doctor. In the UK, the NHS says a GP can discuss support and may refer you for an assessment with a specialist. In the US, the National Institute of Mental Health describes ADHD treatment options and points to SAMHSA's treatment locator. If you are struggling and having thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or contact emergency services where you live. Self-help tips like the ones above can sit alongside professional care; they do not replace it.
