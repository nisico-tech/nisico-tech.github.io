How many units are in a glass of wine, and what do 14 units a week look like?

A 175ml glass of 12% wine is about 2.1 UK units. See units for common drinks, how to calculate them, and what the 14-units-a-week guideline means.

By the Evenline team · Updated 2026-11-02

A standard 175ml glass of 12% wine contains about 2.1 UK units, according to the NHS. A large 250ml glass of the same wine is about 3 units, and a 750ml bottle is about 9. The UK low-risk guideline is not to drink more than 14 units a week on a regular basis, spread over three or more days. That is about six medium glasses of wine or six pints of average-strength beer a week. Here is how to work it out for any drink, and what it means for your evenings.

What is a unit?

In the UK, one unit is 10ml or 8g of pure alcohol. The NHS describes this as roughly the amount the average adult can process in an hour. A unit is a way to compare drinks of different sizes and strengths. It is not a safe amount.

The US uses a different measure. The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) defines a standard drink as any drink containing 14 grams of pure alcohol. Since a UK unit is 8g, one US standard drink is a little under 2 UK units (14 ÷ 8 = 1.75).

How to calculate units yourself

The NHS formula is simple:

units = strength (ABV %) × volume (ml) ÷ 1,000

So a 175ml glass of 12% wine is 12 × 175 ÷ 1,000 = 2.1 units. A 568ml pint of 4% lager is 4 × 568 ÷ 1,000 = about 2.3 units. You can find the ABV on every bottle and can.

What 14 units a week looks like

The NHS advises men and women not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week. If you drink as much as that, it is best to spread it over three or more days, and if you want to cut down, to have several drink-free days each week. In everyday terms, the NHS says 14 units is about 6 pints of average-strength beer or 6 medium glasses of wine.

A few things to keep in mind:

  • It is a "low risk" guideline, not a "safe" one. Drinking less than 14 units does not mean there is no risk.
  • It is not a target. If you drink less than this already, there is no reason to drink up to it.
  • Saving up units for one night is a different pattern. The guideline asks you to spread drinking over several days, not to have 14 units on a Saturday.
  • Other countries use other guidelines and measures. If you live outside the UK, check your national health service's advice.

Why counting helps evening drinkers

Most people underestimate how much they pour at home. Wine glasses are often larger than 175ml, and a top-up does not feel like a new drink. Counting units for a week or two, without trying to change anything, shows your real starting point. From there, small changes are easy to see:

  • Switching from large to standard glasses of the same wine cuts each glass from 3.0 to 2.1 units.
  • Choosing an 11% wine instead of 14% cuts a 175ml glass from 2.5 to about 1.9 units.
  • Two drink-free evenings a week remove those evenings' units entirely.
  • Noting the time of each drink shows when in the evening the extra ones happen, which is often the best place to put your swap or closing time.
Tip: Measure your usual glass once. Pour your normal amount, then tip it into a measuring jug. Use that real number when you count, not the 175ml on the menu.

Cutting down: the NHS basics

If you regularly drink more than 14 units a week, the NHS suggests making a plan and setting a limit before you start, choosing smaller and lower-strength drinks, eating and drinking water first, alternating with non-alcoholic drinks, and taking several drink-free days each week.

When to see a doctor

If you drink heavily every day, do not stop suddenly. Drinkaware explains that heavy drinkers who suddenly stop can get potentially dangerous withdrawal symptoms, and advises speaking to a GP or local alcohol service before reducing or stopping if you might be dependent. Talk to a doctor too if you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant, take medicines that interact with alcohol, or find you cannot keep to the limits you set.
