How to cut down on drinking in the evening

Decide before the evening starts: a limit, a swap, a closing time and drink-free nights. Practical NHS-based steps, plus when to talk to a doctor first.

By the Evenline team · Updated 2026-11-02

The most reliable way to cut down on evening drinking is to decide before the evening starts. Set a limit for tonight, choose what you will have instead of the next drink, and pick a time when the kitchen "closes". Add a few drink-free evenings each week, choose smaller or lower-strength drinks, and look back at your week calmly instead of judging single nights. If you drink heavily every day, do not stop suddenly: talk to a doctor first, because withdrawal can be dangerous.

Safety first: If you get shaky, sweaty or sick when you have not had a drink, or need a drink in the morning to feel better, you may be dependent on alcohol. Drinkaware warns that stopping suddenly can be "dangerous and even life-threatening" for people who may be dependent, and advises speaking to a GP or local alcohol service before reducing or stopping. The tips below are for people cutting down, not for dependent drinkers.

Why the evening is the hard part

For many people, drinking is not spread through the day. It is the glass after work, then the second while cooking, then the third on the sofa. A few things make evenings tricky:

  • Tired decisions. At 9 pm, after a long day, "just one more" is an easy choice. At 5 pm, with a clear head, a limit is easier to set.
  • Habit cues. Opening the fridge, the kids' bedtime, a favourite show: each can trigger the next drink without much thought.
  • No natural stopping point. At home there is no last orders and no bill, so the evening can run on.

Step 1: Make a plan before the evening

The NHS suggests that before you start drinking you set a limit on how much you're going to drink. Do it early, when the decision is easy. A useful evening plan has four parts:

  1. Your limit: "Up to one glass tonight", or "none tonight".
  2. Your swap: what you will have instead of the next one, such as tea, sparkling water or an alcohol-free drink you actually like.
  3. Your closing time: "The kitchen closes at 9:30." After that, no more drinks, whatever the count.
  4. Your reason: one line in your own words: "I want to sleep past 3 am", "I want Saturday mornings back".

Writing the plan as an if-then sentence can help: "If I want a second glass, I will pour the spritz instead." Psychologists call these implementation intentions, and a meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) found that if-then plans had a positive effect on reaching goals across many studies.

Step 2: Change what and how you drink

The NHS cutting-down tips include several small changes that add up:

  • Choose smaller sizes: bottled beer instead of pints, or a small glass of wine instead of a large one.
  • Choose lower-strength drinks: check the ABV on the label. A lower percentage means less alcohol in the same glass.
  • Eat and drink water first, and alternate alcoholic drinks with water or other non-alcoholic drinks.
  • Take several drink-free days each week.

At home, a few practical changes help too: buy smaller bottles, do not keep a stock of open wine, and put the alcohol-free option at the front of the fridge.

Step 3: Have a plan for the "next one" moment

The urge for another drink often comes at the same point each evening. Decide in advance what you will do when it arrives:

  • Pour your swap drink straight away, so your hands have something to do.
  • Read your reason out loud or on your phone.
  • Wait a few minutes before deciding. Change rooms, step outside, run a bath.
  • If you still want it, that is your call. Having it does not cancel the rest of your plan.

Step 4: Plan drink-free evenings

Pick which evenings will be alcohol-free before the week starts, rather than deciding each night. Weeknights with an early start the next day are often the easiest to begin with. If a drink-free evening does not go to plan, swap it for another one later in the week. The week is the unit that matters, not one night.

Step 5: Look at your week, not just last night

A quick check the morning after, "how many last night?", is enough to see patterns over time. Once a week, look back:

QuestionWhy it helps
How many drink-free evenings did I have?Shows progress even in a busy week
Which evenings went over my plan?Points to the real triggers: stress, social plans, no dinner
What helped?The swap, the closing time or the reason you can use again
What will I try next week?One small change, not a new set of rules

When an evening goes over the plan

It will happen. Treat it as information, not failure. Ask what was going on (a stressful day, a party, skipped dinner) and decide one thing you will try next time. Then make tonight's plan as usual. There is nothing to reset and no streak to lose. Shame tends to make the next evening harder, not easier.

When to talk to a doctor

Talk to a GP or doctor if:

  • you get withdrawal symptoms such as shaking, sweating, nausea or anxiety when you cut down;
  • you find you cannot keep to the limits you set, week after week;
  • you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant, or take medicines that interact with alcohol;
  • drinking is affecting your health, work or relationships.

Drinkaware advises getting urgent medical help for a seizure, and medical attention for repeated vomiting, severe shaking or hallucinations. In the US, NIAAA's Rethinking Drinking site has tools for checking your pattern and making a change.
