How to practise ASL fingerspelling (and read it back)

Practical fingerspelling practice for ASL learners: steady letters without bouncing, double letters, reading whole word shapes, and a 10-minute routine.

By the Handwise team · Updated 2026-11-02

To get better at fingerspelling, practise clean, steady letters without bouncing your hand, spell real words you need (names, streets, brands) rather than reciting the alphabet, and spend at least as much time reading fingerspelling as producing it. Read words as whole shapes, not letter by letter. Short daily sessions work better than long ones, and nothing replaces practising with Deaf signers and a Deaf teacher who can tell you what looks natural.

What fingerspelling is for

In ASL, fingerspelling is used in a fairly specific set of situations: mainly proper names (people, places, brands) and words that have no conventional sign, often borrowed from English. Otherwise it is used sparingly (American manual alphabet). That matters for practice: if you fingerspell words that have a common sign, you are practising a habit fluent signers avoid. When you do not know a sign, a Deaf teacher can tell you whether to fingerspell, describe, or learn the sign.

Know the letters that cause trouble

Most letters are a still handshape, but a few need extra attention (American manual alphabet):

  • J and Z move. J is the I handshape with a twist that traces a curve; Z traces a zig-zag with the index finger.
  • Same shape, different orientation. H and U, K and P, and G and Q share a handshape and differ mainly in how the hand is turned. Practise these pairs side by side.

Producing fingerspelling: five habits

1. Do not bounce

Handspeak, an ASL resource by a native signer and ASL instructor, calls bouncing a very common habit among hearing learners and says not bouncing is the first crucial step to efficient fingerspelling (Handspeak: fingerspelling practice and techniques). Keep the hand in one place and let only the fingers change.

2. Rest your elbow if bouncing will not stop

The same page describes a simple drill: rest your elbow on a desk while you fingerspell, which makes bouncing physically harder (Handspeak).

3. Clarity before speed

Fast and blurry is harder to read than slow and clean. Aim for each letter to be fully formed, with an even rhythm across the word. Speed comes with repetition; you cannot force it.

4. Handle double letters properly

Double letters are not just the letter twice. For some letters, a short sideways glide shows the double, as with the N in ANNA; for others, such as the P in APPLE, you form the letter twice. The ZZ in PIZZA is made with bent fingers rather than a single index finger (Handspeak).

5. Spell words you actually need

Your name, your street, your town, your children's names, your favourite brands. These are the words you will really fingerspell, and practising them makes the practice useful from day one.

Reading fingerspelling: the half most learners skip

Many learners can spell their own name smoothly and still freeze when a Deaf person fingerspells a name back at normal speed. Receptive practice is a separate skill and needs its own time.

  • Read whole shapes. Handspeak advises reading fingerspelled words as whole words (configurations), not letter by letter (Handspeak). The overall movement and the first and last letters carry a lot of information.
  • Do not stop to catch every letter. If you missed the middle, let it go and use the context: a name after “my sister” is probably a woman's name.
  • Use real signers on video, at their natural speed. Watching the same short clip several times is fine.
  • Practise with a partner, in person or by webcam (Handspeak). Take turns spelling names from a list the other person has not seen.
  • Ask for a repeat. Asking a signer to spell something again is normal; pretending you understood is not helpful to either of you.
A simple test: spell a list of ten words to your phone's front camera, then watch the recording a day later and write down what you see. If you cannot read your own spelling, others will struggle too.

Small mistakes worth catching early

  • Watching your own hand. Look at the person you are talking to. Your hand will follow.
  • No gap between words. A short pause marks where one fingerspelled word ends and the next begins.
  • Apologising mid-word. If you make a mistake, finish or restart the word calmly; a stream of corrections is harder to read than one clean second try.

A 10-minute daily routine

MinutesWhat
2Warm up: the confusable pairs (H/U, K/P, G/Q) and the moving letters (J, Z)
3Spell five real words you need, elbow resting, no bouncing
4Read fingerspelled words from videos of real signers, whole shapes first
1Spell one new name you will need this week

Respect the people whose language this is

ASL is the language of the Deaf community, and the best learning happens with Deaf people: Deaf teachers, Deaf-led classes, community events where learners are welcome. Apps, videos and books, including ours, are practice aids. If you take an ASL class, your Deaf instructor's feedback on your fingerspelling outweighs any tool. When you join community events, come to communicate rather than to practise on people, and follow the lead of the Deaf hosts.
