Learning ASL as a hearing parent of a Deaf child: where to start

Where hearing parents of a Deaf child can learn ASL: Deaf mentors, family organisations, Deaf-led classes, first signs, and how to respect Deaf community norms.

By the Handwise team · Updated 2026-11-02

If your child is Deaf or hard of hearing and you are hearing, you are far from alone: the National Association of the Deaf says over 90% of these children are born to parents who can hear (NAD). A good start is to learn some ASL now, in short daily bits you can use at home, and to get help from Deaf adults as early as you can: a Deaf mentor programme if one exists where you live, classes led by Deaf teachers, and family organisations. Decisions about your child's hearing, devices and schooling belong with your child's audiologist, early intervention team and you; this guide is only about learning the language.

Why start early

The NAD's position is that the acquisition of language from birth is a human right, and that Deaf and hard of hearing infants should have the opportunity to learn ASL, which it calls a fully accessible visual language, alongside the spoken language their family uses (NAD: early intervention for infants and toddlers). It also says parents should receive accurate information about the benefits of becoming proficient in both ASL and English.

The NAD notes that about 95% of newborns now have a hearing screening before leaving the hospital, but that many babies flagged by screening do not get the follow-up testing needed to confirm their hearing status (NAD). If your baby was referred and you have not had follow-up testing yet, ask your paediatrician or audiologist about it.

Where hearing parents can learn

Deaf mentors at home

The SKI-HI Deaf Mentor Program, established in 1991 and described on a Utah State University page, pairs families of Deaf and hard of hearing children with Deaf adults fluent in ASL. Mentors teach ASL during daily routines, share Deaf culture, invite families to local Deaf community events and serve as language role models for the child, from birth to age six or beyond (SKI-HI Deaf Mentor Program). Availability depends on your state, so ask your state's early intervention programme whether Deaf mentor services exist near you.

Family organisations

  • American Society for Deaf Children (ASDC). ASDC has supported parents of Deaf and hard of hearing children since 1967 and offers online ASL classes and webinars, an ASL stories directory and family events (deafchildren.org).
  • Hands & Voices. A family support organisation that states it does not promote specific communication choices or methodologies, with a parent-to-parent programme called Guide By Your Side (Hands & Voices). Useful if you want to talk with other parents whatever path you choose.

Classes led by Deaf teachers

Community colleges, Deaf community centres and online programmes offer ASL classes. When you compare them, look for Deaf instructors. Learning from native signers gives you natural signing, the facial grammar that is part of ASL, and a direct connection to the community your child will be part of.

What to learn first

Start with the language of your day, not a textbook's vocabulary list:

  • Daily routines: MILK, EAT, MORE, ALL-DONE, SLEEP, BATH, DIAPER, CHANGE.
  • People: MOM, DAD, the names your family uses, and the name signs the Deaf people in your child's life use.
  • Feelings and care: HURT, HAPPY, SAD, LOVE, PLAY.
  • Your own name and your child's name in fingerspelling, until a Deaf person in your circle gives your child a name sign.

Sign while you do the thing: MILK while you hold the bottle, BATH as the water runs. A Deaf mentor or teacher can show you how to make sure your child can see your hands and face, which matters more with a baby than perfect handshapes.

You do not have to be fluent before you start. Signing imperfectly every day, and improving with Deaf adults' help, gives your child more language than waiting until you feel ready.

Honouring the Deaf community

  • Learn from Deaf people. Choose Deaf-led classes and materials where you can, and pay Deaf teachers and mentors for their time.
  • Come to events as a guest. Deaf community events are social and cultural spaces. Arrive to meet people and communicate, follow the hosts' lead, and do not treat people as free tutors.
  • Let name signs come from Deaf people. Name signs are usually given by members of the Deaf community rather than invented by learners, so wait for the Deaf people in your child's life to give one.
  • Your child will have a culture as well as a language. Deaf adults are the best guides to what that can look like; meeting them early helps the whole family.

A realistic weekly rhythm

  • Every day, 10 minutes: five signs for this week's routines, used during those routines.
  • Once a week: a class or a session with a Deaf teacher or mentor.
  • Once a month: a family or community event where Deaf adults are present.
  • Whenever you can: practise reading signing on video, not only producing it.
  • Bring the family in: siblings, grandparents and regular babysitters can learn the same five weekly signs, so your child sees signing from more than one person.
