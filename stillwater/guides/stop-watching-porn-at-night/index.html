How to stop watching porn at night: a plan for the hard hours

Short answer: plan for the hour, not for the whole day. Find the window when it usually happens (for many men it is late at night, in bed, after scrolling), make the first step harder in that window (phone out of the bedroom, a pause on the apps where scrolling turns into porn), and write one concrete "if... then..." plan for the moment the urge shows up. Willpower at 1 a.m. is the weakest tool you have, so take as many decisions as you can at 8 p.m. instead.

Tonight, in five minutes: charge your phone outside the bedroom (or across the room); turn on Screen Time's adult-website filter with a passcode you won't remember easily; write one sentence about why you want to stop and one "if... then..." plan for the moment you reach for the phone in bed.

This guide is self-help, not medical advice. If you feel unsafe or in crisis, call or text 988 in the US, or call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK.

Why nights are the hard part

If your pattern looks like "bed, phone, a few apps, then porn, then 2 a.m. and regret", you are not unusual and you are not broken. Late at night, several things line up at once: you are tired, alone, often stressed or lonely, the phone is in your hand, and the feeds you scroll are built to keep you scrolling.

Research on relapse in other habits points the same way. In the relapse-prevention model developed by G. Alan Marlatt, negative emotional states and conflicts with other people accounted for more than half of the relapse episodes studied, and how a person copes in a high-risk situation predicts relapse better than simply being in that situation (Larimer, Palmer and Marlatt, 1999). In plain words: the situation is predictable, and you can prepare for it.

Step 1: find your window

For one week, don't try to change anything. Just notice:

  • When it usually happens (for example, 23:00 to 01:00).
  • Where (in bed, on the couch, in the bathroom).
  • What came just before (scrolling a particular app, a late message, a bad day, not being able to sleep).
  • How you felt (tired, lonely, bored, stressed, anxious about something tomorrow).

Write it down somewhere private. You are looking for the two or three situations that cover most of the hard moments. Those are the only ones you need a plan for.

Step 2: add friction where the loop starts

Friction means making the first step a little slower and more deliberate. It does not need to be dramatic to help.

  • Phone out of the bedroom. Charge it in another room, or at least across the room, and use a cheap alarm clock. For many people this is the simplest change, because it breaks the whole chain at once.
  • A pause on the gateway apps. If the loop usually starts on one or two social apps, put them behind a pause in your window. On iPhone, Screen Time lets you set schedules and limits for apps (Apple: Set Screen Time schedules and time allowances).
  • Filter adult websites. Screen Time has a built-in "Limit Adult Websites" filter for Safari and supported browsers (Apple: Use Screen Time on iPhone). Our iPhone guide walks through it, including how a Screen Time passcode stops "Ignore Limit" from being a one-tap way out, and what the filter cannot do.

Does a short pause actually change anything? A 2023 study of an app that adds a few seconds of delay before social apps open found that in about a third of opening attempts people closed the app instead, and opening attempts fell over six weeks (Grüning et al., PNAS 2023). The study had no control group and one author created the app, so treat it as encouraging, not proof. The same research found that what mattered most was having an easy way to back out, not a lecture on the screen.

Step 3: write one if-then plan

A goal ("I won't watch porn tonight") is easy to agree with at 8 p.m. and easy to drop at 1 a.m. An if-then plan links a specific situation to a specific action, decided in advance:

  • "If I'm in bed and reach for my phone after 23:00, then I put it on the charger across the room."
  • "If I can't sleep after 20 minutes, then I get up, drink some water and read a paper book in the other room."
  • "If I notice I'm scrolling just to feel something, then I text a friend or step outside for two minutes."
  • "If faith matters to me and I'm tempted, then I put the phone down and take one minute to pray."

These are called implementation intentions, and they are one of the better-studied tools in behaviour change. A large review of many experiments found a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006), and later work showed why: the plan does not make you more motivated, it makes the cue easier to notice and links it to a response you already chose (Webb and Sheeran, 2008). So make the "if" concrete. "If I feel tempted" is too vague; "if I'm in bed with my phone after 23:00" works.

Start with one plan for your most common situation. Add a second only when the first one is a habit.

Step 4: know what to do in the hard minute

Even with a plan, an urge will sometimes show up. Have a short routine ready that does not depend on how strong you feel:

  1. Name it. "This is an urge. I'm tired and lonely." Naming what is going on puts a little distance between you and the feeling.
  2. Read your reason. Keep one sentence, in your own words, about why you are doing this. Read it, don't argue with it.
  3. Breathe for a minute. Slow breathing gives you a minute while the urge passes. In one study, smokers who learned to "surf" urges with mindfulness smoked less over the next week, even though their urges were not weaker (Bowen and Marlatt, 2009). It was a small study on smoking, not porn, but the idea is simple and costs nothing to try.
  4. Change the room. Stand up, put the phone down somewhere else, turn on a light.

You will not always win this minute, and that is fine. The goal is more good nights over time, not a perfect record.

Step 5: get your mornings back

Nights are easier when the day sets them up:

  • A wind-down cue. Pick a time (say 22:30) when the phone goes on the charger and something else begins: a shower, a book, a podcast on a speaker.
  • Deal with the trigger, not just the habit. If loneliness or stress drives most of your hard nights, the long-term fix lives there: a call with a friend, exercise, sleep, talking to someone.
  • Look at the week, not the night. Count the days that went the way you wanted this month. One bad night does not undo them.

If you slip

A slip is information, not a verdict. Ask two questions: what came before, and what would make that moment easier next time? Then adjust your if-then plan. Our guide on what to do after a relapse goes into this in more detail.

When to talk to a professional

Self-help is not enough for everyone. Consider talking to a doctor, therapist or counsellor if you have tried many times and keep losing control, if it is hurting your relationship or your work, if you feel depressed or hopeless, or if you are using porn to cope with something bigger. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 (US) or call Samaritans on 116 123 (UK) now.
