Relapsed on porn? What to do in the next 24 hours, without shame

Short answer: don't restart from zero, and don't spend the day punishing yourself. Write down what came before the slip (time, place, mood, what you were doing on your phone), change one thing about that situation, and get back to your normal day. A slip says something about a situation you can prepare for, not about who you are. Shame feels like taking it seriously, but research on other habits links it to more relapse, not less.

This guide is self-help, not medical advice. If you feel hopeless or unsafe, call or text 988 in the US, or call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK, right now.

First: you are not back at day zero

Many quit-porn communities count streaks, and a slip "resets" the counter to zero. That framing makes a single bad night feel like losing weeks of work, and it is the wrong scoreboard.

Research on streaks in general (not porn specifically) found that people who break a streak tend to disengage from the activity afterwards, especially when they blame themselves for the break (Silverman and Barasch, Journal of Consumer Research, 2023). The counter is designed to motivate you while it grows; when it breaks, it pushes you to quit.

A better scoreboard: days on track this month. If you had 18 good days and 2 hard ones, you had 18 good days. That number does not disappear because of last night.

Second: shame is not the same as caring

Psychologists separate guilt ("I did something I don't want to do") from shame ("I am bad"). Guilt points at a behaviour you can change. Shame points at your whole self, and that is hard to act on.

The difference shows up in research on other habits. In a study of people early in recovery from alcohol problems, those who showed more shame when talking about their last drink were more likely to relapse in the following months (Association for Psychological Science, on Randles and Tracy, 2013). In another study of people in residential treatment, guilt went together with forgiving yourself, while shame went the other way (Subst Abus, 2013).

Relapse-prevention research has a name for the spiral you may be feeling: the abstinence violation effect. When a slip is explained as a fixed, global flaw ("I have no willpower", "I'll never change"), people are more likely to give up altogether. When it is seen as a mistake tied to a specific situation, they are more likely to learn from it and keep going (Larimer, Palmer and Marlatt, 1999).

So the most useful thing you can say to yourself today is something like: "That happened. It was late, I was tired and scrolling. Next time I'll do X."

The 10-minute review

Do this once, today, somewhere private. Keep it short.

  1. What came before? Time, place and what you were doing. Late in bed? Scrolling one particular app? Couldn't sleep? Home alone?
  2. How were you feeling? Tired, lonely, stressed, bored, anxious, angry. Naming it is not an excuse; it's the clue.
  3. Where was the last easy exit? Usually it's much earlier than the moment of the slip: the phone going into bed with you, the first app you opened, the decision to "just check something".
  4. One change. Pick one thing to change at that exit point. Make it concrete, as an if-then plan: "If I'm in bed with my phone after 23:00, then it goes on the charger across the room." Plans phrased like this are one of the better-studied tools for changing behaviour (Webb and Sheeran, 2008).
  5. Then stop. Close the notebook and go on with your day. Going over it again and again is shame wearing a disguise.

The next 24 hours

  • Sleep and eat normally. Tired, hungry evenings are harder ones.
  • Plan tonight. If the slip happened at night, tonight is the night most like it. Decide now where the phone will charge and what you'll do if you can't sleep.
  • Talk to someone if that helps you. A friend, partner, mentor or counsellor. You don't owe anyone a confession, but carrying it alone often feeds the shame. If you tell a partner, our advice is to talk about what you are doing to change, not just about the slip.
  • Don't overcorrect. Sudden extreme rules (deleting every app, "never again" vows, punishments) tend to collapse within days. One small, sustainable change beats a dramatic one.

If you're worried about sex and dating

Many men who slip worry that it has "set back" their sex life. A single slip does not undo the days that went well. If you have ongoing erection problems or other physical symptoms, see a doctor rather than relying on forum advice: they can have medical causes that are worth checking, whatever role porn plays.

Is it "porn addiction"?

You may wonder whether a relapse means you are addicted. That word is used loosely online. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 includes "compulsive sexual behaviour disorder" as an impulse-control disorder, diagnosed when a person has lost control over sexual urges or behaviour for at least six months with significant distress or impairment, and it states that distress that comes only from moral judgement or disapproval is not enough for the diagnosis (Kraus et al., World Psychiatry, 2018).

That matters because feeling "addicted" is strongly linked to how much porn conflicts with your values, not only to how much you watch. In a representative US sample, 11% of men agreed with "I am addicted to pornography", and moral disapproval was among the predictors, alongside religiousness, younger age and how much people watched (Grubbs et al., 2019; see also the moral incongruence review). Neither feeling is wrong. But knowing which one drives your distress helps you choose the right help: practical friction and plans for loss of control, and values work (with or without faith) for the conflict.

When to get more help

Consider a doctor, therapist or counsellor if you have tried for a long time and keep losing control, if it is damaging your relationship or work, if you feel depressed, or if the slips are getting more frequent rather than less. Psychological treatment shows promising results in studies, though the research is still early and of mixed quality (2025 meta-analysis). Self-guided online programs exist too, but many people drop out: in one trial of a free online program, only about one in eight participants completed the final module (Bőthe et al., 2021). Choose something you can actually keep doing.

If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, please call or text 988 (US) or call Samaritans on 116 123 (UK) now. You matter more than any streak.
