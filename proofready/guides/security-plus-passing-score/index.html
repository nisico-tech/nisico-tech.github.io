What is the Security+ passing score, and what does 750 mean?

The Security+ passing score is 750 on a 100-900 scale. What that number means, why it is not a percentage, and how to judge readiness before you book.

By the Proofready team · Updated 2026-11-02

The CompTIA Security+ passing score is 750 on a scale of 100 to 900, for both the current SY0-701 exam and the new SY0-801 (CompTIA, V7; CompTIA, V8). The exam has a maximum of 90 questions in 90 minutes, mixing multiple-choice and performance-based questions. Because the score is scaled, 750 is not the same as “83% correct”, and no practice test can honestly convert your percentage into a 750. What you can do is check whether you would pass on questions you have never seen. This guide explains the numbers and a fair way to judge readiness.

Why 750 is not a percentage

A scaled score is a reported number on CompTIA’s own scale, not a count of correct answers. Three simple facts make a direct conversion impossible:

  • The scale starts at 100, not 0. 750 out of 900 cannot be read as 750 ÷ 900.
  • The number of questions varies. The exam has “a maximum of 90” questions, so the raw total is not fixed (CompTIA).
  • Questions are not all the same kind. Performance-based questions (PBQs) can give partial credit, and scoring accepts different valid approaches (CompTIA, PBQs explained).

So when a practice site shows you “Estimated score: 790”, treat it as that site’s own guess. It is not something CompTIA publishes.

Performance-based questions: what to know

CompTIA describes two kinds of PBQ (source):

  • Simulations: an approximation of a tool such as a firewall or a terminal window. You can skip them and come back later, and they have a reset button.
  • Virtual environments: real virtual machines. You must complete them when you meet them, and there is no reset.

Both can earn partial credit. A practical habit: if a simulation PBQ is eating your time, flag it, answer the multiple-choice questions, then return. Check the on-screen instructions on the day, because what you can skip depends on the type.

Pace: 90 questions in 90 minutes

With a maximum of 90 questions in 90 minutes, your average is about one minute per question, and PBQs usually take longer than that. That means pace is a separate skill from knowledge. Someone who knows the material but reads slowly, or works in English as a second language, can run out of time. Practise under a timer at least some of the time, and notice which kind of question slows you down: long scenarios, “BEST / MOST / FIRST” qualifiers, or PBQs.

How to judge readiness honestly

The trap is the question bank you have memorised. If you have seen a question three times, getting it right shows memory, not understanding. A fairer check:

  1. Use questions you have never seen. Hold back a full practice exam until you think you are close, and take it once.
  2. Look at each domain, not just the total. A good overall score can hide one weak domain.
  3. Check pace separately. Did you finish with time to spare, or did you rush the last 15 questions?
  4. Look for a pattern, not one result. Two good results out of your last three fresh attempts tell you more than one lucky one.
  5. Write down what you expect to score before you start. If you are often surprised, your feeling of readiness is not reliable yet.
Avoid “brain dumps” (leaked real exam questions). Beyond the ethics, they fool you: a high score on remembered questions tells you nothing about the real exam.

Which version should you study for?

CompTIA says SY0-801 launches on 17 November 2026 and SY0-701 retires in English on 11 June 2027 (V7, V8). If you already hold a voucher or have a date booked, check which exam code it is for and study that objectives list. If you are just starting, compare the time you need with the retirement date before you choose.

The week before you book

A short checklist many people skip:

  • Confirm the exam code on your voucher or booking matches the objectives you studied.
  • Do one full timed run in a single sitting, on questions you have not seen. Short sets do not test endurance.
  • Review your “confident and wrong” answers. Questions you were sure about and still missed show the ideas you have mixed up. They are worth more than the ones you guessed.
  • Practise the qualifiers. Many questions turn on one word: BEST, MOST, FIRST, LEAST. Underline it mentally before you read the options.
  • Plan your PBQ time. Decide in advance how many minutes you will give a simulation before flagging it.

If several of these feel shaky, moving the date is usually cheaper than paying for a second attempt.
