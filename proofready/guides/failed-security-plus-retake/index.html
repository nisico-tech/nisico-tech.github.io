Failed Security+? CompTIA’s retake rules and what to do next

Failed the Security+ exam? CompTIA’s retake waiting periods and fees, how to read your result, and a plan to decide when you are ready to book again.

By the Proofready team · Updated 2026-11-02

If you failed Security+, you can book your second attempt straight away: CompTIA requires no waiting period between the first and second attempt. From the third attempt on, you must wait at least 14 calendar days after your most recent attempt. You pay the full exam price each time, and CompTIA offers no free retakes or retake discounts (CompTIA retake policy). Being allowed to retake tomorrow does not mean you should. This guide covers the rules, then a calm way to decide when to book again.

The retake rules in one table

SituationRule
Failed the first attemptNo waiting period before the second attempt
Failed the second attempt or laterWait at least 14 calendar days from your most recent attempt
PriceYou pay the exam price each time you attempt it
DiscountsCompTIA offers no free re-tests or discounts on retakes
Already passedYou cannot retake the same exam code without CompTIA’s prior consent

Source: CompTIA Certification Retake Policy.

Check the exam version before you rebook

Security+ is changing version. CompTIA says SY0-801 (V8) launches on 17 November 2026, and SY0-701 (V7) retires in English on 11 June 2027 (V7; V8). Both have a maximum of 90 questions in 90 minutes and a passing score of 750 on a 100-900 scale. If your next voucher or booking is for a different code than the one you studied, check the objectives for that code before you start your plan.

Step 1: read your result calmly

Your score report is the most useful study document you have now. Before you open any practice test:

  • Write down your score and how far it was from 750.
  • Note the domains or objectives the report says you missed.
  • Write, in two lines, how the exam felt: did you run out of time, freeze on the performance-based questions (PBQs), or misread questions that asked for the BEST or FIRST action?

Those three notes decide the plan. A small gap with one weak domain needs a short, targeted sprint. Running out of time needs pace practice, not more reading. A large gap across everything needs a fuller restart.

Step 2: separate knowledge from pace

The exam allows 90 minutes for up to 90 questions (CompTIA). If you knew the material but did not finish, more videos will not fix it. Practise timed sets, and time yourself on long scenario questions specifically. If you work in English as a second language, practise reading the last sentence of a long question first, so you know what it asks before you read the scenario.

Step 3: train the PBQs

CompTIA explains that simulation PBQs can be skipped and revisited and have a reset button, while virtual-environment PBQs must be completed when you meet them; both can earn partial credit (source). On your next attempt, a time plan helps: decide in advance how long you will give a simulation before flagging it and moving on.

Step 4: measure on questions you have not seen

After a fail, most people go back to the same practice bank. By now you remember many of those answers, so your scores rise even if your understanding does not. To know whether you are really closer:

  1. Keep at least one full practice exam you have never opened.
  2. Study your weak domains first, with new questions.
  3. Take the unseen practice exam under real timing, in one sitting.
  4. Book only when your results on unseen questions are comfortably ahead of where you were, on more than one attempt.
Skip “pass guaranteed” offers and brain dumps. Leaked questions are unethical, and memorised answers are exactly what made practice scores look better than the real exam.

Step 5: pick a date you can keep

Because you pay the full price each attempt (CompTIA), a rushed retake is expensive. The right date is the one your fresh-question results support, not the one that feels urgent. If you booked too early, check your test provider’s rescheduling rules.

Common mistakes after a fail

  • Rebooking the next day out of frustration. The policy allows it, but nothing has changed yet.
  • Restarting the whole course. If the gap was small, rewatching every video wastes the weeks you need for your weak domains.
  • Trusting a practice bank you have finished twice. Scores on remembered questions climb fast and tell you little.
  • Ignoring pace. If you ran out of time, the fix is timed practice, not more notes.
  • Skipping the PBQs in practice. They feel awkward, so people avoid them; that is exactly why they cost marks on the day.
  • Studying alone with no check. Ask a colleague or study group to quiz you on the domains you missed; explaining an idea out loud shows quickly whether you understand it.

And be fair to yourself. A fail on a hard exam is information about where to aim next, not a verdict on your career.
