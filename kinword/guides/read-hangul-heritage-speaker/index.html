How to learn to read Hangul when you already understand Korean

A reading plan for heritage speakers: learn the 24 basic Hangul letters through family words you already know, then read real messages from home.

By the Kinword team · Updated 2026-11-02

If you already understand Korean when your family speaks it, learning to read Hangul is mostly a matter of matching letters to sounds you already know, and it goes faster than for a beginner. Modern Hangul has 24 basic letters, 14 consonants and 10 vowels, grouped into syllable blocks. Learn the letters in a few short sessions, then practise straight away on words you have heard all your life, like 엄마, 할머니 and 밥, instead of textbook words you never use. Your ear will correct your reading.

Why heritage speakers often read less than they understand

Many people who grew up hearing Korean at home understand far more than they can say or read. The language-learning company Kaiwa describes this pattern in its guide for Korean heritage speakers: strong listening, weaker speaking, and Hangul reading and spelling that never fully developed. Some went to weekend Korean school as children; the Center for Applied Linguistics has an overview of Korean heritage language schools in the US. Others never had the chance. Either way, the gap is normal and it says nothing about how “Korean” you are.

The good news is that you are not starting from zero. A beginner has to learn the letter, the sound and the word. You only need the letter: the sound and the word are already in your head.

How Hangul works, in five minutes

  • Letters: 14 basic consonants (ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅅ ㅇ ㅈ ㅊ ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅎ) and 10 basic vowels (ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ ㅣ). There are also five doubled consonants (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ) and combined vowels such as ㅐ, ㅔ and ㅘ.
  • Blocks: letters are written together in syllable blocks. Each block has a starting consonant, a vowel, and sometimes a final consonant underneath, called 받침 (batchim).
  • The silent ㅇ: when a syllable starts with a vowel sound, the block starts with ㅇ, which is silent in that position. That is why 엄마 and 아빠 begin with ㅇ. At the bottom of a block, ㅇ sounds like “ng”, as in 강.
  • History, briefly: Hangul was created under King Sejong and promulgated in 1446 in the text Hunminjeongeum.

A plan that starts from your own words

Step 1: learn the letters with family words

Do not memorise a chart in the abstract. Take words you say or hear every week and use them to learn the letters inside them:

WordYou already know it asLetters it teaches
엄마Mumㅇ (silent), ㅓ, ㅁ, ㅏ
아빠Dadㅏ, ㅃ
할머니Grandmaㅎ, ㄹ as batchim, ㄴ, ㅣ
밥rice, a mealㅂ at the start and as batchim
김치kimchiㄱ, ㅁ as batchim, ㅊ
사랑해I love youㅅ, ㄹ, ㅇ as batchim, ㅎ, ㅐ

Twenty or thirty such words cover every basic letter. Write them by hand once; it fixes the shapes faster than tapping.

Step 2: read slowly, then let your ear take over

Read a word block by block, out loud. The moment it sounds like a word you know, stop spelling and say the word. This is the heritage speaker's advantage: your ear tells you when you got it right. If what you read does not sound like any word you know, check the vowels first; ㅓ and ㅗ, and ㅐ and ㅔ, are the usual suspects.

Step 3: expect spelling and sound to differ

Korean spelling keeps the original form of words, so letters sometimes change sound next to each other. You say 감사합니다 with an “m” sound in the last syllables even though it is written with ㅂ. You already pronounce it the right way; the point is to recognise the written form. When something looks wrong but sounds right, trust your ear and note the spelling.

Step 4: read real things from your life

  • The family group chat. Short, repetitive and personal: 밥 먹었어? (Did you eat?), 언제 와? (When are you coming?), 조심해 (Be careful).
  • Menus and signs at a Korean restaurant or market. You know most dishes by sound already.
  • Song lyrics and subtitles of shows you already watch, with the Korean subtitles on.
  • Your own name and the names of family members in Hangul.

Step 5: write back, in short messages

Reading improves fastest when you also write. Start by replying to the family chat with one short message a day, even 네 (yes) or 고마워요 (thank you). Then two words. Then a sentence. If you are unsure about the tone, our guide on speaking politely to parents and grandparents covers that.

How long does it take?

We will not promise a number of days. It depends on how much Korean you hear, how often you practise and how much you already knew. What we can say is that the letters are the small part: once you know them, everything you read is a word you are likely to recognise, and that makes daily practice feel rewarding instead of a chore.

A good daily habit: five minutes, one real message from family, read out loud. If you can say it, you can read it.

Common sticking points

  • ㅓ vs ㅗ: both are “o”-like to English ears. Say 엄마 and 오빠 out loud and feel the difference in your mouth.
  • Plain, tense and aspirated consonants: ㄱ ㄲ ㅋ, ㄷ ㄸ ㅌ, ㅂ ㅃ ㅍ. You already hear the difference in 달, 딸 and 탈; reading just puts letters on it.
  • Batchim that moves: in 먹었어 the final ㄱ of 먹 is pronounced at the start of the next syllable. Your ear already does this.
  • Feeling slow: reading slower than you understand is expected at first. It catches up.
