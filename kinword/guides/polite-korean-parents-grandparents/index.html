How to talk politely to your parents and grandparents in Korean

Speech levels and honorifics for heritage speakers: when to use 요, 반말 or 드시다, with ready lines for parents, grandparents and older relatives.

By the Kinword team · Updated 2026-11-02

Korean shows respect in two ways at once: the ending of your sentence (the speech level, which depends on who you are talking to) and honorific forms for people you talk about (often your parents and grandparents). For grandparents and other elders, the safe default is the polite 요 ending plus honorific verbs, as in 할머니, 식사하셨어요? (Grandma, have you eaten?). With parents, families differ: many adult children speak casually to their parents, others use 요. Listen to how your siblings and cousins talk to them, and match that.

Speech levels: the ending decides the tone

Korean has several speech levels, each with its own verb endings. In everyday life you will mostly meet three:

StyleExampleTypical use
Formal polite (합쇼체)감사합니다Strangers, work, announcements, speeches
Polite (해요체)고마워요Everyday polite talk, many elders, people you do not know well
Casual (해체, 반말)고마워Close friends, younger siblings, children

Wikipedia's summary notes that the formal polite style is common with strangers and at work, the polite 요 style is a less formal alternative to it, and the casual style is used between close friends and with children (Korean speech levels). Casual speech is often called 반말, and polite speech 존댓말.

This is where heritage speakers often get stuck. If your parents always spoke 반말 to you, as parents usually do with children, then 반말 may be the only Korean you know well. Kaiwa's guide for Korean heritage speakers describes exactly this: family Korean is usually casual, so polite and formal speech is the gap.

Honorifics: respect for the person you talk about

Separately from the ending, Korean marks respect for a senior person who is the subject of the sentence. The main tool is the honorific suffix -시- (or -으시-) added to the verb: 가다 (to go) becomes 가시다 (Korean honorifics).

  • 엄마가 가요. (Mum is going; polite to the listener, but not honorific about Mum.)
  • 엄마가 가세요. (Mum is going; honorific about Mum.)

Some common verbs have their own honorific words instead (Korean honorifics):

PlainHonorificMeaning
있다계시다to be (somewhere)
먹다 / 마시다드시다to eat / to drink
먹다잡수시다to eat (very respectful)
자다주무시다to sleep

The subject particle changes too: 이/가 becomes 께서, and “to someone” (에게) becomes 께 (Korean honorifics). So 할머니께서 주무세요 means “Grandma is sleeping”, said respectfully.

Nouns that change too

A small set of everyday nouns has a respectful version you use for an older person's things. Learner grammar references such as this table of honorific nouns list these pairs:

PlainRespectfulMeaning
밥진지meal
집댁home
나이연세age
생일생신birthday
이름성함name
말말씀words, what someone says

They usually travel with honorific verbs: 할아버지, 진지 드셨어요? (Grandpa, have you eaten?). For a grandparent's birthday, 할머니, 생신 축하드려요 is a warm, correct start. When you hear your parents say 할머니 댁, that is simply “Grandma's home”, said respectfully.

Never use honorifics about yourself. You say 저는 먹었어요 (I ate), not 드셨어요. Honorifics go up, toward the other person.

Ready-to-use lines for family

To grandparents and older relatives

  • 할머니, 식사하셨어요? (Grandma, have you eaten?)
  • 안녕히 주무셨어요? (Did you sleep well? A common morning greeting to elders.)
  • 맛있게 드세요. (Enjoy your meal.)
  • 건강하세요. (Stay healthy; a warm way to end a call.)
  • 할아버지, 보고 싶어요. (Grandpa, I miss you.)

On the phone with a grandparent

  • 할머니, 저예요. (Grandma, it's me.) Note 저, the humble “I”, rather than 나.
  • 잘 지내셨어요? (Have you been well?)
  • 또 전화드릴게요. (I'll call you again.) 드리다 is the humble verb for giving something to an elder, here a call.

Three lines like these are enough to open and close a call in Korean, even if the middle drifts back into English at first.

To parents, if your family uses 요

  • 엄마, 저 지금 출발해요. (Mum, I'm leaving now.)
  • 아빠, 이번 주말에 갈게요. (Dad, I'll come this weekend.)
  • 네, 먹었어요. (Yes, I ate.)

To parents, if your family is casual

  • 엄마, 나 지금 출발해. (Mum, I'm leaving now.)
  • 응, 먹었어. (Yeah, I ate.)
  • 사랑해. (I love you.)

Mixing is common and fine in many families: casual with Mum, 요 with Dad, honorifics for grandparents. When in doubt with someone older, being a bit too polite is rarely a problem; being too casual can be.

How to practise without feeling awkward

  1. Listen for the endings. Next family call, notice whether people end with -요, -니다, or nothing. You already hear it; now name it.
  2. Pick five lines from above that fit your family and say them out loud until they feel natural.
  3. Use one in a real message this week. A short polite message is a low-pressure first step.
  4. Ask a relative how they would say it. Many parents are happy to be asked, and it is a conversation in Korean in itself.

Relatives can sometimes comment on your Korean in ways that sting. Kaiwa's guide mentions that this pressure can make speaking harder. It helps to remember that understanding a language for decades is a real skill, and speaking it is a separate one you are now building.
