How much does a 401(k) contribution reduce your paycheck?

A pre-tax 401(k) contribution lowers your paycheck by less than you put in, because income tax withholding drops too. The math, step by step, and Roth.

By the Pennylab team · Updated 2026-11-02

Short answer: with a traditional (pre-tax) 401(k), your take-home pay drops by less than the amount you contribute. The contribution is taken out before federal income tax is withheld, so your income tax withholding goes down too. Social Security and Medicare are still withheld on the full amount. With a Roth 401(k), the contribution comes out after tax, so your take-home pay drops by roughly the full amount.

The rough formula for a pre-tax contribution, per paycheck:

Drop in take-home ≈ contribution × (1 − your top income tax rate)

where "top income tax rate" is your federal bracket plus your state rate, if your state taxes the contribution the same way. The rest of this guide shows where that comes from and how to check it against your own pay stub.

What a pre-tax contribution skips, and what it doesn't

The IRS says elective deferrals to a 401(k) are not subject to federal income tax withholding at the time of deferral, but they are still counted as wages for Social Security and Medicare (IRS Topic 424).

So on each paycheck:

  • Federal income tax withholding: calculated on your pay minus the pre-tax contribution. It goes down.
  • Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%): calculated on your full pay. They do not change (IRS Topic 751).
  • State and local income tax: in most states the same idea applies as for federal tax, but rules differ, so check your state's treatment.

A designated Roth contribution is made after tax (IRS Topic 424; IRS Roth comparison chart). It does not lower your withholding now; the tax benefit comes later, under the plan's rules.

Compared with contributing nothing: in this example, $138.46 goes into your 401(k), but your paycheck shrinks by about $116. If your plan matches 50% up to 6%, the employer adds about $69 more on top. You can see how the match works in 401(k) match explained.

Three cautions about this kind of estimate:

  1. Withholding is not your final tax. Withholding tables approximate your yearly tax; the real number is settled on your tax return. Your first paychecks can also look different if you started mid-year.
  2. Your bracket may not be one rate. If your income sits near the edge of a bracket, part of the contribution may save tax at one rate and part at another.
  3. Other pre-tax deductions interact. Health insurance premiums paid through a cafeteria plan are generally excluded from wages for income tax withholding and for Social Security and Medicare (IRS Publication 15-B). They lower the base before your 401(k) math, so your stub can differ from a simple calculator.

How to check it on your own pay stub

Once your contribution starts, compare two paychecks (one before, one after) line by line:

  1. Gross pay should be the same.
  2. 401(k) or "pre-tax deferral" line should show your contribution.
  3. Federal taxable wages (sometimes shown as "FIT taxable") should be lower by the contribution.
  4. Social Security and Medicare wages should not change.
  5. Federal income tax withheld should be lower.
  6. Net pay should drop by less than the contribution if you chose pre-tax.

If net pay dropped by the full contribution on a pre-tax election, ask payroll whether it was set up as Roth by mistake. If your withholding looks too high or too low for your situation overall, the IRS offers a free Tax Withholding Estimator and the Form W-4 you would use to change it.

Picking a rate you can live with

The "right" contribution is a personal decision. A few questions people commonly work through:

  • Does the rate capture your full employer match? Check the "up to" number in your formula.
  • What is left for fixed costs? Rent, loan payments and insurance come out of the net pay, not the gross.
  • Do you have a cash buffer? Money in a 401(k) is meant for retirement and can be costly to take out early, under your plan's and the IRS's rules.
  • Can you raise it later? Most plans let you change your rate during the year; the SPD says how and when.
