Why is my first paycheck smaller than I expected? How to read your first pay stub

Your first paycheck is gross pay minus taxes and deductions, and the first one can also be a partial period. Learn each line of a pay stub and what to check.

By the Pennylab team · Updated 2026-11-02

Short answer: the salary in your offer is gross pay. What lands in your account is net pay: gross pay minus federal income tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare, any state or local tax, and deductions you chose, such as health insurance or a 401(k). On top of that, a first paycheck often covers only part of a pay period, because of when you started and when the payroll cutoff was. Reading your stub line by line usually explains the gap.

Step 1: Work out the gross for this paycheck

Divide your annual salary by the number of paychecks in a year:

  • Every two weeks (biweekly): 26 paychecks
  • Twice a month (semimonthly): 24
  • Weekly: 52
  • Monthly: 12

A $60,000 salary paid biweekly is about $2,307.69 gross per paycheck. Semimonthly, it is $2,500.

Then check the pay period dates on the stub. If you started on a Wednesday and the period ran Monday to Sunday two weeks later, your first check covers fewer days. Some employers also pay "in arrears", so your first check may arrive a full cycle after you start.

Step 2: The taxes taken out

Federal income tax withholding

Your employer withholds federal income tax based on the Form W-4 you filled in when you were hired (IRS, About Form W-4). Withholding is an estimate of your yearly tax spread across your paychecks. The IRS's free Tax Withholding Estimator helps you check whether it fits your situation.

Social Security and Medicare (FICA)

These are flat rates on your wages: 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, with your employer paying the same amounts on top (IRS Topic 751). Social Security stops at a yearly wage cap; an extra 0.9% Medicare tax is withheld only on wages above $200,000 in a year. On $2,307.69, that is about $143.08 for Social Security and $33.46 for Medicare.

State and local tax

Depends entirely on where you live and work. Some states have no wage income tax; some cities add their own. Your stub lists each one.

Step 3: The deductions you chose

These are the lines new employees most often forget they agreed to:

  • Health, dental and vision premiums. Usually taken pre-tax through a cafeteria plan, which generally means they also lower the wages used for income tax and FICA (IRS Publication 15-B).
  • 401(k) or 403(b). A traditional (pre-tax) contribution lowers federal income tax withholding but not Social Security and Medicare (IRS Topic 424). If your plan auto-enrolled you, a contribution may appear even if you did not pick one; your plan booklet explains the default and how to change it.
  • HSA or FSA, commuter benefits, life insurance, union dues, and so on.

Some deductions start a cycle or two late, so your second or third paycheck can be smaller than the first. That is normal; check it against your benefits confirmation.

A worked example (example numbers, not advice)

Biweekly, $60,000 salary, full first period, single, a $100 pre-tax health premium and a 6% pre-tax 401(k):

Line Amount Note
Gross pay $2,307.69 $60,000 / 26
Health premium (pre-tax) −$100.00 lowers wages for income tax and FICA
401(k) 6% (pre-tax) −$138.46 lowers wages for income tax only
Social Security 6.2% −$136.88 on $2,207.69
Medicare 1.45% −$32.01 on $2,207.69
Federal income tax varies set by your W-4 and IRS withholding tables
State income tax varies depends on your state
Net pay what is left

The point is not the exact numbers but the order: pre-tax deductions come off first, then taxes are figured on what is left, then any after-tax deductions.

What to check on your first stub

  • Pay period dates and hours (if hourly) match what you worked.
  • Gross pay matches salary ÷ paychecks per year, adjusted for a partial first period.
  • Filing status and W-4 settings shown on the stub are what you meant.
  • State is the right one, especially if you live and work in different states.
  • Benefit deductions match the plan and tier you picked.
  • 401(k) rate and type (pre-tax or Roth) match your election.
  • Year-to-date figures start from zero (new job) and add up.

If something is off, ask payroll in writing. Federal law requires employers to keep accurate records of hours and pay, though the federal wage law itself does not require giving you a pay stub; that depends on your state (U.S. Department of Labor FAQ).

When your paycheck changes later in the year

Net pay can move even when your salary does not:

  • A deduction starts late, such as a benefit that begins after a waiting period.
  • You change your W-4, your 401(k) rate or your benefits at open enrollment.
  • You get a raise, which changes both gross pay and withholding.
  • Social Security stops for the year once your wages pass the yearly wage base, which only affects higher earners (IRS Topic 751).

Each time, compare the new stub with the previous one line by line, the same way as above.

Your next decision: the 401(k) rate

For many people the first paycheck is the moment they realise the 401(k) rate they picked during onboarding changes what lands in their account. A pre-tax contribution lowers take-home pay by less than its size, and contributing at least up to your employer's match formula means you get the full match.
