401(k) match explained: how much to contribute to get the full match

A 401(k) match is money your employer adds when you contribute. How to read your match formula, find the rate that gets all of it, and check vesting.

By the Pennylab team · Updated 2026-11-02

Short answer: an employer match is money your employer adds to your 401(k) when you contribute from your own paycheck. The formula in your offer letter or plan booklet tells you how much they add and up to what point. To get the whole match, you contribute at least up to the limit in that formula (for example, 6% of pay for a "50% up to 6%" match). Below that rate, you leave part of the match unclaimed. Whether you also keep the match if you leave the job depends on your plan's vesting schedule.

This guide shows how to read the formula, do the math on your own salary, and check the details that most often surprise people in their first job.

Where to find your match formula

Look in three places, in this order:

  1. Your offer letter or benefits overview. It usually has one line such as "100% match on the first 4% of pay".
  2. The summary plan description (SPD). The U.S. Department of Labor describes it as the plan booklet you should receive when you enroll, written to be easier to read than the legal plan document. Plans must give it to you within 90 days of becoming a participant (DOL, What You Should Know About Your Retirement Plan).
  3. Your benefits portal. The enrollment screen often repeats the formula next to the contribution slider.

If the three disagree, the SPD and the plan document win. Ask HR in writing if anything is unclear.

How to read the formula

Match formulas have two parts: how much the employer adds per dollar you put in, and up to what share of your pay they do it.

  • "50% up to 6%": for every dollar you contribute, the employer adds 50 cents, on contributions up to 6% of your pay. Vanguard uses this as its example of a typical plan design (Vanguard research, 2026).
  • "100% up to 3%": dollar for dollar, on the first 3% of pay.
  • Tiered, e.g. "100% of the first 3%, then 50% of the next 2%": dollar for dollar on the first 3% of pay, then 50 cents per dollar on the next 2%. You need to contribute 5% to get all of it.

The "up to" number is the one that matters for your decision: it is the lowest contribution rate that captures the full match.

Worked example (example numbers, not advice)

Say your salary is $60,000 a year and the formula is 50% up to 6%.

You contribute You put in per year Employer adds per year Match left unclaimed
0% $0 $0 $1,800
3% $1,800 $900 $900
6% $3,600 $1,800 $0
10% $6,000 $1,800 $0

Going from 6% to 10% puts more of your own money in, but the employer adds nothing more. Going from 3% to 6% doubles what the employer adds. That is why the "up to" number is a natural first checkpoint.

What this table does not show is what each rate does to your take-home pay, which is often the real worry in a first job. A traditional (pre-tax) contribution lowers your paycheck by less than the amount you contribute, because it also lowers your income tax withholding. We walk through that in How much does a 401(k) contribution reduce your paycheck?.

Four details that change the answer

1. Vesting: when the match is really yours

Your own contributions are always 100% yours. The employer's match may vest over time. Under federal rules, a plan's match must vest at least as fast as one of two schedules: 100% after 3 years of service ("cliff"), or a graded schedule that reaches 20% after 2 years and 100% after 6 (IRS, Retirement Topics: Vesting). Many plans vest faster, some immediately. If you might leave within a couple of years, check your schedule in the SPD.

2. Per-paycheck matching

Some plans calculate the match each pay period. If you contribute a large share early in the year and hit the annual limit before December, you could miss the match on later paychecks, unless the plan does a year-end "true-up". The SPD says which one your plan does. If it does not say, ask.

3. Traditional or Roth

Many plans let you choose pre-tax (traditional) or after-tax (Roth) contributions. Pre-tax contributions are not subject to federal income tax withholding when you make them, while Roth contributions are taxed when earned (IRS Topic 424; IRS Roth comparison chart). The match formula usually applies the same way to both. Which one suits you depends on your tax situation now and later, which is a question for your own research or a tax professional.

4. The annual limit

For 2026 the IRS limit on what you can contribute from your pay to a 401(k) is $24,500, with higher catch-up limits for people 50 and older (IRS, Nov. 13, 2025). In a first job this rarely binds, but it matters for the per-paycheck point above.

A short checklist before you enroll

  • Write down your match formula exactly as the SPD states it.
  • Find the "up to" rate: the lowest rate that captures the full match.
  • Check the vesting schedule and how long you expect to stay.
  • Check whether the match is per paycheck and whether there is a true-up.
  • Choose traditional or Roth (or a mix), if your plan offers both.
  • Before you confirm, see what the rate does to your take-home pay and to the cash you keep for rent and emergencies.
  • After your first paycheck with the contribution, compare the stub to what you expected.
