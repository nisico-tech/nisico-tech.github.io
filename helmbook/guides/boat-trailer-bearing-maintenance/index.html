How often should you service boat trailer bearings?

Dexter says to lubricate trailer bearings every 12 months or 12,000 miles. Why bearings matter, what to check before a trip, and how to track the trailer.

By the Helmbook team · Updated 2026-11-02

Dexter, a major trailer axle maker, says trailer wheel bearings should be lubricated every 12 months or 12,000 miles, and recommends repacking them annually with fresh grease (Dexter). Like engine service, that is a “whichever comes first” rule, but it runs on miles and months, not engine hours. Your axle or trailer maker’s manual has the exact interval for your trailer, and boat trailers that go into the water may need more attention. Here is why bearings matter, what a repack involves, and how to keep track.

Follow your trailer and axle manufacturer’s manual. If you are not confident doing the work, have a trailer or marine shop do it; a wheel that comes off on the highway is dangerous for everyone.

Why bearings deserve a place in your log

In its July 2025 report, BoatUS listed the top reasons for its roadside Trailer Assist calls: flat tires or no spare at 44%, wheel bearing failure at 21%, trailer axle at 11%, trailer failure at 6% and tow vehicle failure at 5% (BoatUS). Tires and bearings together make up about two-thirds of those calls. Both are cheap to check and easy to forget, because a trailer sits unused for long stretches.

For most recreational boat trailers, the year arrives long before 12,000 miles, so think of it as an annual job, best done before the season. Dexter also suggests inspecting your brakes when you do bearing maintenance (Dexter).

What repacking involves

Dexter’s guide describes repacking a bearing by hand (source):

  1. Put a small amount of grease, about the size of a half-dollar, in your palm.
  2. Press the wide end of the bearing into the edge of the grease so grease is forced into it.
  3. Turn the bearing and work across each roller in turn until it is full.
  4. Check that grease comes through the rollers.
  5. Coat the bearing cup lightly with grease before reinstalling.

Removing the hub, cleaning out the old grease, inspecting the parts and reassembling correctly are covered in Dexter’s service manual. If you have not done it before, ask someone experienced to show you once.

A quick check before each trip

These checks take a few minutes and need no tools beyond a tire gauge:

  • Tires: pressure (cold) against the number on the tire or trailer label, visible cracks, and the condition of the spare. You do have a spare?
  • Hubs: after the first stop, carefully feel each hub with the back of your hand. One that is much hotter than the others is worth a closer look.
  • Noise and play: a grinding sound, or a wheel that rocks when you push the top and bottom of the tire, needs attention before a long drive.
  • Lights and connections: brake lights and turn signals, the coupler latch, safety chains and the brake breakaway cable if fitted.

Water and boat trailers

Boat trailers are backed into water, sometimes salt water, and then often parked for weeks. Water and salt are hard on bearings and brakes. Your axle maker may give a shorter interval for trailers that are regularly submerged; check its manual and follow the shorter one if you launch often, especially in salt water.

How to keep track of the trailer

The trailer does not have an hour meter, so its log is different from the engine’s:

  • Write the date of each bearing service and the tow vehicle’s odometer reading (or an estimate of trip miles).
  • Note the next due point as “12 months or 12,000 miles” from that service, or whatever your manual says.
  • Record tire details, including the date code on the sidewall, so you know their age.
  • Keep receipts for bearings, seals and tires with the boat’s record. A buyer will ask.

Questions owners often ask

My trailer sat all winter. Do the bearings still count the year?

Yes. A “12 months or 12,000 miles” rule counts time even when the trailer is parked (Dexter).

Can I just add grease through the cap?

Some axles have fittings for adding grease without taking the hub apart, and some do not. Whether that replaces a full repack depends on your axle, so check the axle maker’s manual for your model.

What about the tires?

Flat tires or no spare were the single biggest reason for BoatUS Trailer Assist calls in its 2025 report (BoatUS). Check pressure before each trip, look for cracks, and know the age of your tires from the date code on the sidewall. If you are unsure whether a tire is safe, ask a tire shop.

Who should do the work?

Repacking is a common owner job, but it has to be done correctly. If you have any doubt, a trailer or marine shop can do it, and you can still keep the record.
