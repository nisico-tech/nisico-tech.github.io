Boat engine service: by hours or by date, which comes first?

Outboard service intervals are usually hours or months, whichever comes first. How that works, why the date often wins, and how to keep a simple log.

By the Helmbook team · Updated 2026-11-02

For most boat engines, service is due after a set number of engine hours or a set time, whichever comes first. Suzuki’s owner’s manual puts it plainly: “Maintenance intervals should be judged by number of hours or number of months, whichever comes first” (Suzuki manual, via Crowley Marine). Yamaha’s outboard chart uses columns like “After first 20 hrs”, “Every 50 hrs” and “Annually or 100 hrs” (Yamaha). So if you use your boat lightly, the date often arrives before the hours do. Your own engine’s manual is the only source for its exact schedule; this guide explains how to read and track it.

Intervals differ by brand, model and engine type, and even between manuals of the same brand. Always follow your engine and trailer manufacturer’s manual, and ask a qualified marine technician if you are unsure.

How “whichever comes first” works

Each service task has two limits: hours and time. You keep track of both from the last time the task was done, and the task is due as soon as either one runs out.

Example (sample numbers)Hours since last serviceTime since last serviceWhich comes first?
Weekend boater, task every 100 h or 12 months45 h12 monthsThe date
Charter or guide boat, same task100 h5 monthsThe hours
Boat laid up all season, same task0 h12 monthsThe date (time still counts)

The last row surprises people. Many parts age even when the engine does not run, which is why manuals pair hours with months.

What a typical outboard chart looks like

Yamaha’s public maintenance chart for four-stroke outboards lists items such as engine oil, the oil filter, fuel/water separating and on-engine fuel filters, spark plugs, gearcase lube, the impeller/water pump assembly, power trim and tilt fluid, and storage items like fogging oil (Yamaha). It also says to flush the engine “after each use, especially in salt or brackish water”, and to refer to your owner’s manual or an authorised dealer for your engine’s specific requirements.

Suzuki’s manual for the DF25A/DF30A gives a first service at 20 hours (1 month), including replacing the oil filter (source). Many engines have a similar early service after the first hours of a new engine, so check yours.

Why keeping on top of it matters

BoatUS reported 111,928 requests for on-water towing and assistance in its July 2025 release. Mechanical breakdown (engine, transmission, drives) was the top reason at 54%, followed by running aground at 12%, battery or electrical issues and suspected fuel issues at 9% each, and engine overheating at 5% (BoatUS). Not every breakdown is a missed service, but regular servicing is the part you control.

How to keep a simple maintenance log

A notebook works. A spreadsheet works. What matters is that it answers three questions quickly: what is due next, what was done last time, and when. For each entry, record:

  • Date of the work.
  • Engine hours at the time, and which meter you read (a dash meter and the engine’s own computer can show different numbers, so always use the same one, or note which).
  • Which engine, if you have more than one (port, starboard, kicker).
  • What was done: “oil and filter changed”, “impeller replaced”, “anodes inspected, OK”.
  • Parts and fluids used, if you know them.
  • Who did it and the cost, with receipts kept together.

Then, for each recurring task, write the next due point as both an hour reading and a date. Example: “Gear oil: next at 545 h or 1 May, whichever first.”

Five habits that make the log useful

  1. Log hours at the end of each trip, or at least each month in season. A log you only update at service time cannot warn you.
  2. Write the source of the interval next to each task (“Owner’s manual p. 56”), so you know it is right for your engine.
  3. Do not copy intervals between engines. An outboard, a sterndrive and a diesel inboard can have very different schedules, even on the same boat.
  4. Include the trailer. Its bearings and tires run on miles and months, not engine hours (see our guide on trailer bearings).
  5. Keep the history when you sell. A clear record of what was done and when is easier for a buyer or surveyor to read than a box of receipts.

Questions owners often ask

My engine only did 30 hours this year. Do I still need the annual service?

If your manual says “100 hours or annually, whichever comes first”, then yes: the year has run out even though the hours have not. If you are unsure what your manual means for your use, ask your dealer.

The dash and the engine show different hours. Which do I use?

Pick one source and stick with it, and write down which one it is. If you ever switch, note both readings on the same day so the history still makes sense.

Do I need a log if a dealer does all my servicing?

It still helps. Dealers keep their own records, but you may change dealers, sell the boat or need the history quickly. Ask for an itemised invoice each time and add it to your log.
