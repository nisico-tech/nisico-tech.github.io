How much protein do you need on a GLP-1? A simple way to work it out

Protein targets proposed for adults on semaglutide or tirzepatide, a table by body weight, and practical ways to eat enough when your appetite is low.

By the Holdline team · Updated 2026-11-02

Expert groups suggest that adults on a GLP-1 medicine aim for at least the standard 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day, and a joint advisory from four US obesity and nutrition societies notes that higher targets of 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg a day have been proposed during active weight loss (ACLM, ASN, OMA and TOS joint advisory, 2025). For a person weighing 80 kg (176 lb), that higher range is roughly 96 to 128 grams a day. Protein works best alongside strength training, and your own target is something to agree with your prescriber or a dietitian, especially if you have kidney disease or another condition.

Why protein matters more on a GLP-1

When you lose weight, part of what you lose is lean mass, which includes muscle. The joint advisory lists muscle and bone loss among the challenges of GLP-1 treatment, and says that eating more protein alone is probably not enough: it recommends pairing treatment with regular strength training at least 3 times a week, plus at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week (joint advisory).

The practical problem is appetite. Many people on these medicines simply eat less of everything. In data from adults using a food-logging app, presented at the European Congress on Obesity in 2026, people using a GLP-1 ate less protein, carbohydrate and fat than non-users, with a similar mix, and nearly 88% of GLP-1 users did not meet the recommended protein intake (Healio, April 2026). Smaller portions mean protein has to be a deliberate choice, not an accident.

How to work out your number

  1. Take your weight in kilograms (pounds divided by 2.2).
  2. Multiply by 0.8 for the general adult baseline, and by 1.2 and 1.6 for the higher range proposed during weight loss.
  3. Bring those numbers to your prescriber or dietitian and agree a range that fits you.

These are arithmetic, not prescriptions. Your prescriber or dietitian may set a different target for you, for example based on a different reference weight, so ask how they work it out.

Getting enough when you are not hungry

Put protein first: eat the protein part of a meal before the rest, while your appetite is still there.
  • Spread it out. Several smaller protein servings across the day are easier than one large one when you feel full quickly.
  • Start with breakfast. Many breakfasts are low in protein. Eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese or a protein shake can change the day's total.
  • Use dense foods. Greek yogurt, skyr, cottage cheese, eggs, fish, chicken, tofu, tempeh, lentils and beans give a lot of protein for the volume.
  • Drink some of it. On days when solid food is hard, a milk-based drink or a protein shake may be easier. Check with your prescriber if you have nausea that stops you eating or drinking.
  • Read labels, not front-of-pack claims. Look at grams of protein per serving, and the serving size.
  • Keep a few easy options at home for low-appetite days, so the choice is already made.

A simple way to keep track

You do not need to weigh every meal. A rough daily total is enough to see whether you are usually near your range or usually far below it:

  • Note the protein in grams from the label, or use a typical value for foods without one.
  • Look at the week, not the single day. One low day is normal; a low week is worth noticing.
  • Write down which meals got you closest to your range and repeat them.

Do not forget strength training

Protein gives the body building material; strength work is the signal to keep muscle. You do not need a gym to start. Sit-to-stand from a chair, wall push-ups and rows with a resistance band are common starting moves. Start light, stop if something hurts, and ask your doctor first if you have heart or joint problems. The joint advisory's target is strength training at least 3 times a week, but any regular routine is a better start than none.

Other nutrients to keep an eye on

Eating less overall can also mean less fibre, fluid and some vitamins and minerals. The joint advisory lists micronutrient deficiencies among the issues clinicians should watch for during GLP-1 treatment (Obesity Medicine Association summary). If you are eating much less than before, ask your prescriber whether any blood tests or supplements make sense for you.

Common questions

Do I need protein powder?

No. It is one convenient option, not a requirement. Whole foods work if you can eat enough of them.

Is more protein always better?

Not necessarily. The higher range is what has been proposed during active weight loss for most adults. People with kidney disease and some other conditions may need a different target, so agree yours with a professional.

Should I keep tracking protein after I stop the medicine?

Many people keep the habit after stopping, since appetite usually returns and it helps to already have routines in place. Whether you keep a specific target is something to review with your prescriber.
