What happens when you stop a GLP-1? What the studies show and how to prepare

Weight regain is common after stopping semaglutide or tirzepatide, but the amount varies a lot. What trials and real-world studies show, and how to prepare.

By the Holdline team · Updated 2026-11-02

When people stop semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic) or tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro), most regain some of the weight they lost, usually gradually over the following year rather than all at once. How much varies a lot from person to person. In clinical trials, where people stopped the medicine and the trial's lifestyle support at the same time, a large share of the loss came back within 12 months. In everyday practice the picture is milder, partly because many people restart or switch treatment. The most useful thing you can do is decide with your prescriber how you will stop, and keep watching your own trend so you notice early.

What the trials found

Two well-known trials took people off the medicine and followed them for about a year:

  • Semaglutide (STEP 1 extension). After 68 weeks on semaglutide, treatment and the trial's lifestyle programme both ended. One year later, people had regained 11.6 percentage points of the weight they had lost, leaving them 5.6% below where they started (Wilding et al., Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, 2022). That is roughly two-thirds of the loss back within a year.
  • Tirzepatide (SURMOUNT-4). People who had lost at least 10% on tirzepatide were switched to placebo. After one year, 82% of the 308 people on placebo had regained more than a quarter of what they lost. But the spread was wide: 54 regained less than 25%, 77 regained 25-50%, 103 regained 50-75% and 74 regained 75% or more (American College of Cardiology summary of the post hoc analysis).

A 2026 review in The BMJ pooled 37 studies with 9,341 people. On average, people regained about 0.4 kg (just under 1 lb) a month after stopping weight-loss medicines and were projected to be back at their starting weight in under two years. Regain was faster than after diet and activity programmes. Only eight of those studies looked at the newer GLP-1 medicines, with at most 12 months of follow-up after stopping (BMJ Group press release).

What happens outside trials

Real life looks different. A Cleveland Clinic study of nearly 8,000 adults who stopped semaglutide or tirzepatide within 3 to 12 months of starting found that, on average, they did not regain much in the following year, and 45% kept the weight off. The authors point out that many people restarted the same medicine or moved to another obesity treatment, which helps explain the gap with trials (Cleveland Clinic, March 2026).

Stopping is often not a single event. Many people pause, lower the dose, switch, or restart. Plan for the version of "stopping" you are actually doing.

Why weight comes back

GLP-1 medicines reduce appetite while you take them. When the effect fades, many people notice hunger and "food noise" coming back. In the studies above, weight came back month by month rather than in one jump, which is why a trend you check regularly is more useful than a single weigh-in.

How to prepare before you stop

1. Make the plan with your prescriber

Whether to stop, taper or switch, and how fast, is a medical decision. Ask what they suggest for your situation, what you should watch for, and when they want to see you again. If you are in England, national quality standards say people stopping weight-management medicines should be given advice on maintaining changes and support for their health and wellbeing (NICE QS212, statement 7), so it is reasonable to ask what that support looks like for you.

2. Build the habits while you are still on treatment

It is easier to start eating enough protein and doing regular strength work while your appetite is quiet than after it returns. A joint advisory from four US obesity and nutrition societies recommends strength training and adequate protein during GLP-1 treatment to help protect muscle (ACLM, ASN, OMA and TOS joint advisory, 2025). Nobody has yet shown which after-stop programme works best, so treat these as sensible foundations, not a guarantee.

3. Pick the number you will watch

Choose a weight range you would be comfortable staying in, rather than one exact number. Weigh in at a regular rhythm that does not stress you (many people use once or twice a week) and look at the trend over several weeks.

4. Write an "if this, then that" plan

Decide now what you will do if your trend moves above your range for a few weeks: for example, book an appointment, add one more protein-rich meal a day, or restart a strength routine you dropped. A plan written in calm times is easier to follow than one invented under stress.

5. Keep your records in one place

Your dose history, side effects, weight trend and notes are what your prescriber needs if you later discuss restarting or switching. Keep them, even after the last dose.

When to talk to a professional

Talk to your prescriber before stopping, and again if your weight rises quickly, if you have diabetes or another condition the medicine was treating, or if eating starts to feel out of control. If you have a history of disordered eating, ask for support before you stop.
