Your GLP-1 coverage is ending: a checklist for the next 30 days

Insurance stopping cover for your GLP-1? What to confirm, what to ask your prescriber, appeals, other ways to pay, and how to keep your records.

By the Holdline team · Updated 2026-11-02

If your insurance is about to stop covering your GLP-1 medicine, use the time before the end date to do four things: confirm exactly what is changing and when, ask your prescriber what your options are, check whether you can appeal or get help with the cost, and keep a full record of your treatment so far. Do not change or stop your dose on your own because coverage is ending; that is a decision to make with your prescriber. This checklist walks through each step.

Why this happens to so many people

Cost is a common reason people stop. In a November 2025 KFF poll, 56% of adults who had taken a GLP-1 said it was difficult to afford, about a quarter of insured users paid the full cost themselves, and cost was one of the most commonly cited reasons for stopping, alongside side effects (13%) (KFF Health Tracking Poll). Employer plans, formularies and public programmes change every year, so a coverage end date is often outside your control.

Step 1: Find out exactly what is changing

  • Get it in writing. Ask your plan or HR for the notice that explains the change: which medicine, which use (diabetes or weight management), and the date.
  • Check the reason. A medicine may be dropped entirely, moved to a higher cost tier, or covered only with prior authorization or for certain diagnoses. Each has different next steps.
  • Ask about alternatives on the formulary. The plan may still cover a different medicine in the same class. Your prescriber can tell you whether that makes sense for you.
  • Note the date somewhere you will see it, and count back 30 days to start the steps below.

Step 2: Book a conversation with your prescriber

Go in with questions, not a decision. Useful ones:

  • What are my options if coverage ends: a covered alternative, a different dose or form, self-pay, or stopping?
  • What should I expect if my treatment changes or ends, and what follow-up should I arrange?
  • If I restart later, what would that look like?
  • Is there anything you would like me to track between now and then?

Bring a short summary of your treatment: start date, doses over time, side effects, and your weight trend. It makes the appointment faster and the advice more specific.

Coverage ending is not the same as stopping. Many people move to another option rather than stop. Decide what you are doing with your prescriber first, then plan around it.

Step 3: Check whether you can appeal

If a claim or a prior authorization is denied, US health plans must let you appeal. There are two levels: an internal appeal to the insurer, and then an external review by an independent third party, which means the insurer no longer has the final say (HealthCare.gov: appealing a health plan decision). Your prescriber's office can often help with the letter of medical necessity. Note that an appeal applies to a decision about your claim or coverage; it may not help if your employer has removed a benefit from the plan altogether. Ask your plan which situation you are in.

Step 4: Look at other ways to pay

  • Medicare. From July 1, 2026 to December 31, 2027, the Medicare GLP-1 Bridge demonstration gives eligible Part D beneficiaries access to certain GLP-1 medicines with a $50 copay (CMS: Medicare GLP-1 Bridge). Check with your plan whether you are eligible.
  • Manufacturer self-pay programmes. The main manufacturers sell some of their medicines directly to people paying cash, for example NovoCare from Novo Nordisk; Eli Lilly runs a similar programme called LillyDirect. Prices and the doses offered change often, so check the current terms yourself.
  • Other forms of the same medicine. Some medicines now come as tablets or other forms with different prices and coverage. Ask your prescriber whether any is suitable for you.
  • Be careful with unregulated sellers. Buy only through a licensed pharmacy with a valid prescription.

This is not financial advice. Compare the total cost over a year, not only the first month, since introductory prices can rise.

Step 5: Keep your records

Whatever you decide, your history matters. If you later restart, switch, or change insurer, a clear record of doses, side effects and weight over time helps your next prescriber. Before the end date:

  • Export or save your dose log and weight history.
  • Write down which doses you tolerated and which side effects you had.
  • Keep copies of prior authorizations, denial letters and appeal letters.

Step 6: Decide what you will track after

If you do stop or pause, studies show weight tends to come back gradually over the following months, though how much varies a lot between people (BMJ Group summary of a 2026 review of 37 studies). Choose a weight range you are comfortable with, a weigh-in rhythm that does not stress you, and what you will do if your trend leaves the range for a few weeks, such as booking an appointment. Keeping up protein and strength habits you built on treatment is a reasonable foundation, though no after-stop programme has been proven to prevent regain.

WhenWhat to do
30 days beforeGet the change in writing. Book your prescriber.
3-4 weeks beforeAppointment: options, questions, a plan. Start an appeal if one applies.
2 weeks beforeCheck self-pay, Medicare or other options and their total yearly cost.
1 week beforeSave your records. Pick what you will track after.
After the dateKeep your routine and check your trend. Follow up with your prescriber as agreed.
