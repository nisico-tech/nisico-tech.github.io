How much protein do you need in perimenopause and menopause?

The published protein ranges for adults, older adults and people who exercise, turned into grams, with simple ways to spread protein across the day.

By the Midform team · Updated 2026-11-02

There is no official protein target specific to perimenopause or menopause. The standard Recommended Dietary Allowance is 0.8 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day; an expert group on older adults recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kilogram for people over 65; and the International Society of Sports Nutrition suggests 1.4 to 2.0 g per kilogram for most people who exercise to build or keep muscle, spread across the day in servings of about 20 to 40 g. If you strength train, the higher ranges are the ones written for you. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor before eating more protein.

General nutrition information, not medical or dietary advice. If you have kidney disease, diabetes or follow a special diet, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing how much protein you eat.

Why protein matters in midlife

Osteoporosis Canada explains that protein forms the structure in bone that holds calcium and is important for keeping muscles strong, and that weaker muscles can affect balance. It adds that protein intake is associated with greater bone mass and fewer fractures when calcium intake is adequate.

These are simple multiplications of the published figures. One practical approach if you strength train is to start in the lower half of the exercise range and see whether it fits your appetite, budget and the way you like to eat. If your weight is well above the healthy range, ask a dietitian which weight to use in the calculation.

Spread it across the day

The International Society of Sports Nutrition suggests servings of about 0.25 g per kilogram of body weight, or 20 to 40 g, ideally spread evenly every three to four hours. In practice that means a real portion of protein at breakfast, lunch and dinner, and perhaps a snack, instead of most of it at dinner.

Turning a daily number into meals

Divide your daily figure by the number of times you eat. For example, a 100 g target over four eating times is about 25 g each time, which sits inside the 20 to 40 g serving range above. A 90 g target over three meals is 30 g per meal. This is simple arithmetic, but it turns a big daily number into something you can check at each meal.

If you eat little or no meat

Osteoporosis Canada's list of protein foods includes eggs and milk products, beans, lentils and tofu, nut and seed butters, shelled nuts and seeds. A vegetarian or mostly plant-based diet can reach the same targets; it usually just needs a protein food planned into every meal rather than left to chance. Labels on tofu, tempeh, soy drinks and protein yogurts show how much each serving gives.

Easy swaps

  • Breakfast: Greek yogurt, eggs, cottage cheese or a tofu scramble instead of toast or cereal alone.
  • Lunch: add a palm-sized portion of chicken, fish, beans or lentils to a salad or soup.
  • Snack: a glass of milk, a cheese portion, a handful of nuts or a protein yogurt.
  • Dinner: build the plate around the protein first, then add vegetables and carbohydrates.

To know how many grams a food gives you, read the Nutrition Facts label: protein is listed per serving. After a week of checking labels, you will probably find it easier to estimate by eye.

Do you need protein powder?

No, food can cover it. A powder is just a convenient way to add a serving when a meal falls short, for example after a workout or at a busy breakfast. If you use one, check the label for the protein per scoop and for anything you prefer to avoid.

What protein will not do

Protein supports the muscle you build through training; it does not build muscle on its own, and it is not a treatment for menopause symptoms. Strength comes from the training, done consistently and progressively, with enough protein and sleep to recover.

Questions for your doctor or dietitian

  • Is there any reason I should not eat more protein, for example my kidney function?
  • Which body weight should I use to calculate my target?
  • Do I need calcium or vitamin D as well, given my bone health?
