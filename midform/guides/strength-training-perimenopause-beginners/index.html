How to start strength training in perimenopause: a beginner's guide

How to start lifting in perimenopause: what the guidelines say, five movements to learn, how heavy to go, a sample week and when to see your doctor first.

By the Midform team · Updated 2026-11-02

Start with two or three full-body strength sessions a week, each 30 to 40 minutes, built on five basic movements: squat, hip hinge, push, pull and carry. Pick a weight you can lift with good form, add repetitions first and weight later, and keep a simple log. Public health guidelines in the US and UK ask adults to do muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week, working all the major muscle groups, on top of 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. Nothing about perimenopause stops you from starting, but check with your doctor first if you have osteoporosis, a joint problem or another health condition.

General fitness information, not medical advice. It does not cover HRT or any treatment, and no training plan can promise to change your symptoms.

What the guidelines actually say

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends that adults get 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week that work the legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms. The NHS guidelines say the same about strength on at least two days a week and list examples such as lifting weights, working with resistance bands and body-weight exercises like push-ups.

The European Menopause and Andropause Society (EMAS), in its 2026 exercise factsheet, describes resistance exercise as a way to build lean muscle and bone density, and says exercise at the menopause should be undertaken to maximise bone strength and reduce the risk of falls. It is also honest about the limits: the evidence on exercise for hot flushes and night sweats is mixed. Train to get stronger, not because you were told it will fix symptoms.

A single pair of dumbbells is enough to start at home. In a gym, the same movements work with machines or a barbell.

How heavy, how many

For beginners the safest rule is technique first, load second. Choose a weight you could lift a few more times than you are asked to, with steady control on the way down. A simple starting structure is two or three sets of 8 to 12 repetitions. If you have low bone density, the UK consensus statement “Strong, steady and straight” suggests building up over time to loads you could lift about 8 to 12 times, around three sets, two or three days a week, starting lighter and learning the technique first.

How to progress without guessing

  1. Write down the weight and repetitions for every set.
  2. When you finish all sets at the top of your range (for example 3 sets of 12) with good form, add a little weight next time and drop back to 8 repetitions.
  3. If you only have one pair of dumbbells, progress with more repetitions, a slower lowering phase (count three seconds down) or a harder version of the movement.
  4. If a set hurts in a joint (not the normal effort in the muscle), stop that exercise and use an easier swap.

A sample beginner week

  • Monday: sit-to-stand or goblet squat, glute bridge, wall push-up, band row, carry. Two or three sets each.
  • Wednesday: the same session. Try to beat Monday by one repetition on one exercise.
  • Friday: the same again, or a 20-minute version if the week was hard.
  • Other days: walking or any aerobic activity you enjoy, working towards the 150 minutes in the guidelines.

Repeating the same session for the first few weeks is deliberate: it lets you learn the movements and see your numbers go up.

Bad nights and bad days

Poor sleep, low energy or a sore joint can make you want to skip a workout. Another option is a lighter session: the same exercises with one set fewer, the same weight, and longer rests. You keep the habit, and you do not have to guess what to do on the next good day. If something hurts sharply or pain persists, stop and get it checked.

Pelvic floor and impact

EMAS notes that menopause and ageing are risk factors for pelvic floor dysfunction. If you notice leaking, heaviness or pressure when you lift, jump or cough, breathe out on the effort rather than holding your breath, keep to low-impact options and ask your doctor about seeing a pelvic floor physiotherapist. The same applies before adding jumping if you have osteoporosis: the UK consensus advises individual advice after spine fractures.

Protein

Strength training and enough protein go together. There is no menopause-specific official number; see our guide on how much protein you need in perimenopause and menopause for the published ranges.

When to talk to your doctor first

  • You have osteoporosis or have had a fracture from a minor fall.
  • You have a heart condition, high blood pressure or another long-term condition.
  • You have joint pain that changes how you move, or pelvic floor symptoms.
  • You are recovering from surgery or an injury.
