How to practise reading at home with a child who struggles

A calm ten-minute routine for practising reading at home with a child aged 6 to 9 who finds it hard: what to do, what to say when they are stuck, what to avoid.

By the Blendwise team · Updated 2026-11-02

Practise a little every day, in the same order your child's tutor or teacher uses: a few sounds, blending, some words, then a short decodable text, about ten minutes in total. Let your child do the reading, ask them to sound out words they do not know instead of guessing, praise the effort of sounding out, and re-read the same text the next day. Accuracy comes first; speed comes later. Keep it short and calm, and stop before it turns into a battle.

This is practice advice for parents, not a diagnosis. If reading stays very hard despite regular practice, talk to your child's teacher about an evaluation, and keep your tutor in the loop.

Why short, daily practice

The US practice guide Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade from the Institute of Education Sciences rates the evidence as strong for teaching children to decode words and to link the sounds of speech to letters, and as moderate for making sure each child reads connected text every day. England's reading framework adds that most children need to decode a word several times before they can read it at a glance, and that becoming fluent takes a considerable investment of time. A struggling reader needs many accurate repetitions, and a short daily session is an easy way to add them at home.

A ten-minute session, step by step

StepTimeWhat it looks like
1. Sounds2 minShow the letters or letter groups taught recently (for example sh, ch, a, i, o). Your child says the sound, not the letter name.
2. Blending2 minYour child says each sound in a word and blends them: sh-i-p, ship.
3. Words3 minA short list of words using only taught patterns, plus one or two irregular words the tutor has taught.
4. Decodable text3 minA few sentences or a short story built from the same patterns.

On a tired night, do steps 1 and 4 only. A short session is better than none, and a missed night is fine.

Your role: the listener

The reading framework describes what an adult does when listening to one child read: choose a text closely matched to the sounds the child knows, show interest in it, expect the child to sound out unfamiliar words, and praise them for doing so. The adult can re-read a sentence to help the child keep track of the story and explain what a word means. The next day, they might re-read the same book so the child reads more words at a glance.

What to say when your child gets stuck

  • Point, do not tell. Point to the first sound and say “What sound is this?” Then “Now say all the sounds and push them together.”
  • Do not ask them to guess from the picture or the first letter. The framework describes decodable text as a way to avoid exactly that.
  • Two tries, then move on. If a word still will not come, say it, ask your child to point to each sound as they repeat it, and note it for the tutor.
  • Mixed-up letters such as b and d are worth noting each time so the tutor can see the pattern.

What not to do

  • Do not time them or push for speed. The framework is clear that urging children to read faster will not make them fluent; they can read only at the speed they can decode.
  • Do not jump ahead to sounds the tutor has not taught yet.
  • Do not turn mistakes into a score your child sees.

Keeping it in the tutor's order

If your child sees an Orton-Gillingham or other structured literacy tutor, ask three questions each week: which sound or pattern is the focus, which irregular words were taught, and is there a decodable text you can use at home. Practising exactly what was taught keeps home and lessons pulling in the same direction.

Keep a simple record

A notebook is enough: the date, minutes practised, words that were hard, and the type of mistake (a vowel swapped, a letter reversed, a sound skipped, a guess from the first letter). Bring it to the tutor or to a school meeting. It turns “we practise most nights” into something specific the professional can use.

Keep reading for pleasure too

Practice time is not the only reading in the day. Read aloud books your child loves, well above their decoding level, and talk about them. Hearing stories you cannot yet read yourself is part of why reading is worth the effort.

When to ask for more help

Talk to your child's teacher or tutor if your child is not keeping up with the sounds being taught, if reading causes distress most nights, or if you are unsure whether the school's approach matches what the tutor is doing. Ask what support is available and whether an evaluation is appropriate. A practice routine at home works best alongside the right teaching, not instead of it.
