What are decodable books, and how do you choose them for a struggling reader?

What makes a book decodable, why it helps a child who finds reading hard, and how to pick books that match the sounds your child has been taught.

By the Blendwise team · Updated 2026-11-02

Decodable books are books written in careful steps so that a child can sound out every word using only the letter-sound patterns they have already been taught. For a child who finds reading hard, they are the practice books that match what the tutor or teacher is teaching right now: no guessing from pictures, no words full of patterns they have not met yet. Choose them by matching the book to the sounds your child already knows, not by age, reading level or colour band.

This guide is about practice at home. It does not diagnose dyslexia or any learning difference. If you are worried about your child's reading, talk to their teacher or tutor and ask about an evaluation.

What makes a book decodable

England's Department for Education describes decodable books, in its guidance The reading framework, as books “structured in cumulative steps” for children learning to read, so that as their knowledge of the alphabetic code grows they can decode every word. The framework quotes the national curriculum, which says children should read aloud books that are consistent with their developing phonic knowledge and that do not require them to use other strategies to work out words.

In practice that means three things:

  • Only taught patterns. If your child has learned short vowels and the sounds sh and ch, a decodable story uses words like ship, chop and fish, not words like light or phone.
  • A planned order. Phonics programmes organise their decodable books in the order the sounds are taught, so each book builds on the last.
  • No guessing required. The framework points out that with decodable text children are not forced to guess from pictures, the context, the first letter or the shape of a word.

Why they matter for a struggling reader

A child who is still decoding word by word needs a lot of accurate practice. The framework says decodable books help children feel successful from the start, and that practising with them speeds up the time needed to reach the accuracy required to read a wide range of children's books. It also says that older pupils who have not yet learned to decode well need such books too, so a nine-year-old reading decodable text is not doing something babyish; they are practising at the right level.

In the US, the Institute of Education Sciences practice guide Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade rates the evidence as strong for teaching students to decode words, analyse word parts, and write and recognise words, and as moderate for making sure each student reads connected text every day. Decodable stories are one way to give a beginning reader daily connected text they can actually read.

How to choose the right book

  1. Ask for the sound list. Ask your child's tutor or teacher which letter-sound patterns and which irregular words have been taught so far. Structured programmes follow a set order, and your tutor will know where your child is in it.
  2. Match the book to the list. Check whether the series lists which sounds each book uses; if it does not, compare a page with your list. Pick books that use only taught patterns.
  3. Run alongside or a little behind. The framework recommends that the decodable books children read run alongside or a little behind the teaching, so they always feel a sense of achievement. If this week's new sound is ch, a book that practises last week's sounds plus a few ch words is ideal.
  4. Do a quick check. Open a page and ask your child to read it. If they can sound out nearly every word with effort, it is a good practice book. If they stall on many words containing patterns they have not learned, the book is ahead of them.
  5. Be careful with levels and colours. The framework warns against levelled or colour-banded books that include words not aligned with the phonics programme or that expect children to guess from the first letter, pictures or context. A “Level C” label tells you nothing about which sounds are inside.

Re-reading is not cheating

Children often want to read the same decodable book again. That is useful. The framework explains that most children have to decode a word several times before they can read it “at a glance”, and that re-reading a text gradually increases the number of words they recognise at a glance. It also says that urging children to read faster will not increase fluency: they can read only at the speed they can decode. Re-read for accuracy first; speed follows.

Keep reading aloud to them

Decodable books are for practising decoding, not for everything. Keep reading interesting books aloud to your child so they hear rich vocabulary and stories well above what they can decode yet. Both kinds of reading matter: one builds decoding, the other builds language and the wish to read.

When to move on

The framework says children who are behind should keep reading decodable books until they can blend sounds to read new words fluently and automatically. Your tutor or teacher is the right person to judge when that point has come.
