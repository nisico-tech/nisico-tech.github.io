How to take a history (Anamnese) in German for the Fachsprachprüfung

A clear question order for the FSP patient conversation, lay terms patients use and their medical terms, and phrases for when you did not understand.

By the Sprechzeit team · Updated 2026-11-02

In the first part of the Fachsprachprüfung you take a full history (Anamnese) from a simulated patient in about 20 minutes, then explain your suspected diagnosis in words the patient understands. What examiners listen for is language: do you understand the patient without asking back all the time, do you cover the history in a clear order, and do you explain things in plain German? The safest way to prepare is a fixed order of questions you can say without thinking, plus a list of everyday expressions patients use and the technical term you write down for each.

Formats differ by Land. The order below follows the content named in the official Ärztekammer Westfalen-Lippe leaflet: personal details, current complaints, previous illnesses, medication and family history, then suspected diagnoses explained to the patient. Check your own chamber's sheet.

A history order you can say in your sleep

  1. Opening and personal details: „Guten Tag, mein Name ist Dr. Haddad, ich bin Assistenzarzt hier. Wie heißen Sie, und wie alt sind Sie?“
  2. Current complaint: „Was führt Sie heute zu uns?“
  3. Details of the complaint: since when, where, how it feels, how strong, what makes it better or worse, anything else with it.
  4. Previous illnesses and operations: „Haben Sie Vorerkrankungen? Wurden Sie schon einmal operiert?“
  5. Medication: „Welche Medikamente nehmen Sie regelmäßig ein?“
  6. Allergies: „Haben Sie Allergien, zum Beispiel gegen Medikamente?“
  7. Family history: „Gibt es in Ihrer Familie Herz- oder Krebserkrankungen?“
  8. Social history: work, living situation, smoking, alcohol.
  9. Closing: suspected diagnosis, next steps, questions.

Many courses add a block of general questions (sleep, appetite, weight, digestion), often called vegetative Anamnese. Whether you need it depends on the case; having the questions ready costs nothing.

Patients do not speak textbook German

The simulated patient may answer in everyday language, sometimes with dialect colour. Your job is to understand it, keep talking to the patient in plain words, and write the technical term in your notes for Parts 2 and 3. Practise pairs like these:

In the third part some chambers also ask you to give the everyday German word for common technical terms (ÄKWL), so practise the list in both directions.

When you did not understand

Asking the patient to repeat is part of real medicine. What matters is that it does not happen at every sentence. Useful phrases:

  • „Entschuldigung, das habe ich nicht ganz verstanden. Können Sie das noch einmal sagen?“
  • „Meinen Sie damit, dass …?“ (checking your understanding)
  • „Habe ich Sie richtig verstanden: Die Schmerzen haben heute Morgen angefangen?“

A short summary in the middle of the history („Ich fasse kurz zusammen …“) shows the examiners that you followed, and gives you a moment to check your notes.

Explaining the suspected diagnosis in plain German

The Niedersachsen information sheet says candidates must be able to inform patients about findings and a diagnosis, describe the further course, and explain the pros and cons of a planned measure and its alternatives (NiZzA). In practice that means plain sentences:

  • „Ich vermute, dass die Beschwerden vom Herzen kommen. Das müssen wir genauer untersuchen.“
  • „Wir machen zuerst ein EKG und nehmen Blut ab.“
  • „Haben Sie dazu noch Fragen?“

Avoid saying only „Verdacht auf ACS“ to the patient; save that for your colleague in Part 3.

Three habits that make the conversation easier

  • One question at a time. „Rauchen Sie, und trinken Sie Alkohol?“ invites half an answer. Ask, wait, then ask the next.
  • Open first, then closed. Start with „Erzählen Sie mal …“ and narrow down with yes/no questions only when you need a detail.
  • Stay with „Sie“. Keep the polite form throughout, even if the patient is chatty or uses dialect.

Notes that help Parts 2 and 3

Notes are allowed in Westfalen-Lippe, are not marked, and must be handed in at the end (ÄKWL). Divide your page into the same blocks as your history order before the patient starts talking. Write technical terms, numbers and times, not sentences. When you write the documentation, you then only have to turn each block into German sentences.

A practice routine

  • Learn the history order by heart, out loud, until you can say it in two minutes without notes.
  • Each day, run one case with a partner who plays the patient in everyday German, with a 20-minute timer.
  • Write the documentation from your notes straight after, then present the case in three to five minutes.
  • Keep a list of every lay expression you did not understand and its technical term.
