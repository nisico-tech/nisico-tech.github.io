What happens at the English proficiency (ELP) roadside check?

How the roadside English proficiency check works for truck drivers: the interview, the four road signs, what is not allowed, and how to prepare out loud.

By the Roadside Ready team · Updated 2026-11-02

At the roadside English language proficiency (ELP) check, an inspector talks to you in English about ordinary things (where you are going, what you haul, your licence and logs). If you cannot answer, you are placed out of service. If you can, the inspector may then ask you to explain the meaning of four road signs, and you need at least three right. Since 25 June 2025 a failed check can put you out of service, and you cannot use an interpreter, a phone app or cue cards during it. This guide explains each step with the official sources, so you know what to practise.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules and procedures can change and can differ by state. If you have received a violation, talk to your carrier’s safety department or a qualified professional.

The rule behind the check

The federal rule is 49 CFR 391.11(b)(2). As the Oregon Department of Transportation’s safety notice on ELP summarises it, drivers must be able to read and speak English well enough to talk with the general public, understand highway traffic signs and signals in English, respond to official inquiries, and make entries on required reports and records.

The rule is old, but enforcement changed in 2025. FMCSA rescinded its 2016 policy and, from 25 June 2025, a driver who fails the ELP assessment is cited and placed out of service under the CVSA out-of-service criteria (Trucking Dive; Oregon DOT). In December 2025 the US Transportation Secretary said about 9,500 truck drivers had been placed out of service for English-language violations that year (FreightWaves).

Step 1: the questions every inspection starts with

Most drivers never get a formal assessment. According to the Oregon notice, inspectors normally start any inspection with simple questions, such as:

  • “Where are you coming from?”
  • “Where are you going to?”
  • “What company do you work for?”
  • “Can I get your CDL?”
  • “What are you hauling?”

If you answer these with little or no difficulty, the notice says you meet the requirement and nothing more is needed. If you cannot understand and respond, the inspector moves to the official assessment.

Step 2: the interview

In the official assessment, the inspector tells you to answer in English and asks a set of questions. Oregon’s procedure says to pick seven questions, to rephrase if the driver struggles, and to give the driver time to reply. Trucking Dive, reporting FMCSA’s May 2025 guidance, lists the kinds of topics: where the trip started and where it ends, your time on duty, your logbook and driving time, your licence, your shipping papers, and the truck’s equipment (source).

If you cannot answer the basic interview questions, you are placed out of service and the sign test is skipped.

What the interview is not

  • It is not about your accent. Oregon’s notice tells inspectors directly that the assessment has nothing to do with accents, and that it is the inspector’s duty to listen closely and ask clarifying questions.
  • It is not a fluency exam. The questions are about your trip, your documents and your truck: things you deal with every day.
  • It is not a team effort. If you have a co-driver, Oregon tells inspectors that the co-driver must not interrupt or answer for you, and may be separated from you.

Step 3: the road signs

If you pass the interview, the inspector checks that you understand US highway signs, including changeable (electronic) message signs. In Oregon’s procedure, the inspector picks four signs from a list of examples and asks you to explain what each one means. To pass, you need to explain at least three of the four satisfactorily (Oregon DOT).

US signs follow the national Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD), published by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA), so the same shapes and messages appear in every state.

What you cannot use during the check

AidAllowed during the assessment?
Interpreter or translatorNo
Phone or translation appNo (Oregon asks drivers to hang up and set the phone away; no earpiece)
Cue cards with written answersNo
Co-driver answering for youNo

Sources: Trucking Dive on FMCSA’s guidance (interpreters, cue cards and smartphone apps are barred) and the Oregon notice (phones, earpieces, co-drivers). Oregon’s FAQ adds that once a driver has been placed out of service, the inspector may use an interpreter or app so the driver clearly understands the decision.

How to prepare

  1. Say your answers out loud. Reading a list is not the same as answering a person. Practise full, short sentences: “I am going to Dallas, Texas.” “I am hauling frozen food.”
  2. Know your own facts in English. Your company name, your home terminal, today’s origin and destination, your load, and how long you have been driving today.
  3. Learn the document words. CDL, medical card, logbook or ELD, bill of lading, registration. Be able to say “Here is my…” for each.
  4. Practise explaining signs in your own words. You do not need the exact text of the manual; you need to say what the sign tells you to do.
  5. Practise asking for a repeat. “Can you repeat that, please?” and “Can you say it slower?” are normal English and show you are listening.
  6. If you are a CDL student in Texas: since 1 June 2026, Texas gives all CDL and commercial learner permit knowledge tests in English only, and interpreters are prohibited (Texas DPS). Check your own state’s DMV for its current rules.
