Your rescue dog's first 30 days: what to do, week by week

A calm plan for a newly adopted dog: the first three days, week one and weeks two to four, what's normal, and when to call your vet or a behaviourist.

By the Settle team · Updated 2026-11-02

In the first 30 days with a rescue dog, go slowly. Take your dog straight home, give them a quiet space of their own, keep the house calm and the routine predictable, and keep walks short and close to home. Do not force contact with you, children or other pets. Expect hiding, a poor appetite, accidents and broken nights at first; these are common. Use rewards, never punishment, and call your vet or a qualified behaviour professional if anything worries you. Settling can take weeks, and often months.

Before day one

  • Set up a safe space: a bed or crate in a quiet corner, away from the front door and busy walkways, with water nearby.
  • Ask the shelter what your dog eats and buy the same food. The Scottish SPCA suggests continuing the same food, amount and feeding pattern the dog had in their care and making any change of diet gradually over five days.
  • Agree on house rules with everyone at home: which rooms, which furniture, who feeds, who walks. Consistency helps the dog feel secure.
  • Clear your calendar for the first days. No parties, no long trips, no big family visits.

Days 1-3: arrive and decompress

The Scottish SPCA recommends you take your dog straight home on adoption day and warns about "trigger stacking", when lots of small stresses add up without time to recover. Going home is exciting for you, but your dog does not yet know it is safe.

  • Let them choose contact. Give free access to their safe space. If they approach you, great; if they hide, let them.
  • Walks can wait. The SSPCA notes that on the first day dogs don't necessarily need a walk at all. Over the first few days, use short, regular walks near home so they learn the area.
  • Take them out often for the toilet, especially after eating, drinking, playing or resting, and reward them when they go outside.
  • Keep the first night simple. Many dogs are unsettled at first. A bed near where you sleep, a quiet house and a last toilet trip can help. Expect to be woken.

Week 1: build a routine

The SSPCA says the main priority in the first week is getting your dog settled into a routine and comfortable with the people at home. It suggests watching four things: are they eating OK, are their toileting habits normal, are they sleeping enough, and what do they like and dislike.

  • Feed, walk and rest at roughly the same times each day.
  • Take care at the front door. The SSPCA suggests keeping the dog away from the door, or on a lead, when you answer it at first.
  • If visitors come, introduce them outside first so you can see whether your dog is comfortable.
  • If you have another dog, give them breaks from each other, feed them separately and introduce toys slowly.
  • Start short alone-time practice: a few minutes in another room, then build up slowly (see below).

Weeks 2-4: expand slowly

As your dog eats well, toilets normally and sleeps well, you can expand what you do: new walking routes, a quiet park, one calm visitor at a time. The SSPCA's advice is that it is always better to go too slowly than too quickly, and that it can take weeks and typically months for a dog to fully settle.

This is also a good time to start gentle training with rewards: their name, a hand target, coming to you for a treat. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) says reward-based methods should be used both for everyday skills and for unwanted behaviour, and that aversive methods relying on force, pain or discomfort should not be used. It lists tools and techniques to avoid, including choke, prong and shock collars, shouting and leash jerks.

Tip: Keep a simple log for the first month: what they ate, toilet trips, any scares and what calmed them. A few lines a day show progress you would otherwise miss, and give your vet or behaviourist real information if you need help.

Alone time in the first month

Some newly adopted dogs struggle when left. The ASPCA says separation anxiety is far more common in dogs adopted from shelters than in dogs kept by one family since puppyhood. Start with very short absences, keep departures and returns low-key, and give a stuffed food toy as you leave. See our guide on leaving a rescue dog alone for the first time for step-by-step help.

When to get professional help

  • Call your vet for anything that looks like illness or pain, or for any of the warning signs in the table above.
  • Get a qualified behaviour professional if your dog growls, snaps or bites, panics when left, or is very fearful. In the UK, the SSPCA points to the Animal Behaviour and Training Council register. In the US, the ASPCA suggests a Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist or a board-certified veterinary behaviorist.
  • Call your shelter or rescue. Many offer post-adoption support. Asking is not a failure.

How Settle helps

Settle is being built as a day-by-day guide for exactly this month: a few small steps each day, what is normal and what is not, a quick log, and calm plans for the first night, the first walks, alone time and visitors.
