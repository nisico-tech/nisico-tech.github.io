How to leave a rescue dog home alone for the first time

Build alone time from seconds, not hours. A gentle step-by-step plan for newly adopted dogs, the signs of separation anxiety, and when to get help.

By the Settle team · Updated 2026-11-02

To leave a newly adopted dog home alone, build up slowly from absences so short your dog does not get upset: a few seconds out of sight, then a minute, then a few minutes, adding time only when your dog stays calm. Keep leaving and coming back low-key, give a long-lasting food toy as you go, and avoid long absences until your dog can cope with short ones. If your dog panics, barks non-stop, destroys things near doors or tries to escape when alone, talk to your vet and a qualified behaviour professional.

Why rescue dogs can find being alone hard

A rescue dog has just lost everything familiar: people, place and routine. The ASPCA says separation anxiety is far more common in dogs adopted from shelters than in dogs kept by one family since puppyhood, and that changes of guardian, schedule, home or household can trigger it. That does not mean your dog will have it, but it is a good reason to teach alone time gently from the start.

Signs your dog is struggling when alone

The ASPCA lists signs that appear when the dog is alone or separated from you, and usually stop when you are there:

  • toileting indoors;
  • persistent barking or howling;
  • chewing, digging or destroying things, often near doors and windows;
  • trying to escape, sometimes with injury;
  • pacing in a fixed pattern.

Some dogs also get anxious as you get ready to leave: pacing or whining when you pick up keys or put on a coat. The ASPCA also advises seeing your vet first to rule out medical causes, such as a urinary infection, for accidents indoors.

Step-by-step: building alone time

The method below follows the gradual approach the ASPCA describes. The golden rule: keep each absence shorter than the time it takes your dog to get upset.

  1. Start with you out of sight, not out of the house. Step behind a door for a few seconds, then come back calmly.
  2. Add a food toy as you leave. A stuffed chew or food puzzle links your leaving with something good.
  3. Wait until your dog is calm before the next practice, and keep greetings low-key when you return.
  4. Move to very short trips outside. Out of the front door for a few seconds, then back.
  5. Add time slowly, often only seconds at a time at first. If your dog copes, add a little. If not, go back a step.
  6. Practise departure cues without leaving. Pick up your keys, put on your shoes, then sit down again, so these stop predicting an absence.

A sample first week

This is an example of pace, not a schedule your dog must keep. Every dog is different; go by your dog's reactions, not the calendar.

Watch for stress signs: the ASPCA lists dilated pupils, panting, trembling and pacing. If you see them, the absence was too long. Shorten it next time. Going back a step is part of the plan, not a failure.

Make the place they stay a good place to be

Where your dog waits matters as much as how long you are gone. A few practical ideas:

  • Use the same quiet spot they already rest in, not a room they only see when you leave.
  • Take them out first for a toilet break and, later in the first weeks, a short walk.
  • Keep it comfortable: water, their bed, something that smells of you, and nothing they can chew that would hurt them.
  • Keep returns boring. Come in, put your bag down, then greet calmly once your dog has settled.

Managing real life while you train

The hard part is that life does not stop while you practise. The ASPCA notes that in a full desensitisation plan, the dog must not be left alone outside of training sessions. Ways people manage this:

  • take time off work in the first days after adoption;
  • ask a friend, family member or dog sitter to stay with your dog;
  • use doggy day care if your dog copes well with other dogs;
  • take your dog with you where dogs are welcome.

What not to do

  • Do not punish. The ASPCA explains that these behaviours are distress, not disobedience or spite, and punishment can make the problem worse.
  • Do not use aversive tools. AVSAB advises against shock collars, prong and choke collars, and intimidation such as shouting or squirt bottles.
  • Do not rush. A long absence that goes badly can set training back.

When to get professional help

Desensitisation is detailed work, and the ASPCA recommends a trained professional: a Certified Applied Animal Behaviorist or a board-certified veterinary behaviorist, or a certified trainer with specific experience of fear. In the UK, the Scottish SPCA points adopters to the Animal Behaviour and Training Council register. Talk to your vet first, especially about medical causes. Medication is something only your vet or a veterinary behaviourist can decide on.

Get help quickly if your dog hurts themselves trying to escape, if barking or destruction goes on for long periods, or if you are thinking about returning your dog. Your shelter or rescue may offer post-adoption support.
